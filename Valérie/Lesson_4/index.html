01 / CALIFORNIA CALLING

A world of food in one city

Learn country names, food adjectives, cuisine categories and useful restaurant vocabulary.

GRAMMAR · LEARN BEFORE YOU PRACTISE

Countries → nationalities → cuisines

1 · WHY / WHEN

Choose the correct way to describe a cuisine

We use nationality adjectives to say what kind of food we like. First identify the country; then change the country name into the adjective.

2 · HOW TO BUILD IT

Statement: I like Italian food. Negative: I don’t like spicy food. Question: Do you like Thai food? Answer: Yes, I do / No, I don’t.

3 · EXCEPTIONS & COMMON MISTAKES

Country and cuisine are not interchangeable: “Italy food” ✗ → “Italian food” ✓. Some names change a lot (Greece → Greek; Thailand → Thai); all nationality words start with capitals.

4 · IMPROVE YOUR ANSWER

I like food from Japan → I like Japanese food.

Explore the examples and details below, then try the guided exercises.

The country is a place

Italy, Japan, Mexico, China, India, Thailand, Vietnam, Greece, France, Korea, Ethiopia and the United States are countries.

The adjective describes the cuisine

Italy → Italian food; Japan → Japanese food; Mexico → Mexican food; China → Chinese food; India → Indian food; Thailand → Thai food.

More useful combinations

Vietnam → Vietnamese; Greece → Greek; France → French; Korea → Korean; Ethiopia → Ethiopian; United States → American; Spain → Spanish; Lebanon → Lebanese.

Sentence patterns

Positive: I like Italian food. Negative: I don’t like very spicy food. Question: Do you like Japanese food? Choice: Would you prefer Mexican or Thai food?

Articles and capitals

An Italian restaurant, a Thai restaurant. Nationality adjectives start with capital letters. Say “food from Italy” OR “Italian food”, not “Italy food”.

Food preferences

I prefer seafood. I would like a vegetarian dish. I can’t eat nuts. Does this dish contain dairy? Use does + subject + base verb.

Cuisine adjectives describe a culinary tradition, not necessarily the origin of the restaurant owner or every dish.

GRAMMAR · LEARN BEFORE YOU PRACTISE

Compare restaurant styles

1 · WHY / WHEN

Compare options before making a decision

Use a comparative for two options and a superlative for one item in a group of three or more. Short adjectives generally add -er/-est; longer ones use more/most.

2 · HOW TO BUILD IT

Statement: A café is cheaper than a steakhouse. Negative: The steakhouse isn’t cheaper. Question: Which is the cheapest? Choice: I’d choose the café because it’s quieter.

3 · EXCEPTIONS & COMMON MISTAKES

big → bigger (double the consonant); easy → easier (y → ier); good → better → the best; bad → worse → the worst. “More cheaper” ✗ and “the most cheapest” ✗.

4 · IMPROVE YOUR ANSWER

Casual restaurant → more relaxed than a formal steakhouse.

Explore the examples and details below, then try the guided exercises.

Comparative: two choices

Fast food is usually quicker than a formal dinner. A steakhouse can be more expensive than a casual restaurant. Short adjective: -er; long adjective: more + adjective.

Superlative: a group

Which option is the cheapest? Which is the most formal? Use the + -est or the most + long adjective.

Useful irregular forms

good → better → best; bad → worse → worst. Never “more better”.

Negative and question

The restaurant isn’t cheaper than the café. Is the steakhouse more expensive than the seafood restaurant?

Step 1 · Reserve your table

Make a phone reservation before arriving.

GRAMMAR · LEARN BEFORE YOU PRACTISE

Time, party size and polite booking questions

1 · WHY / WHEN

Make a polite request by phone or online

“I would like” is polite for stating what you want; “Could I…?” and “Do you have…?” ask whether something is possible. For a refusal, give a short, natural reply rather than forcing “would not like.”

2 · HOW TO BUILD IT

Statement: I’d like to reserve a table. Negative preference: I’d prefer not to sit outside. Question: Could I book a table for two? Reply: Yes, certainly / I’m sorry, we’re fully booked.

3 · EXCEPTIONS & COMMON MISTAKES

After could: base verb (“Could I book…?”), not “Could I to book…?”; after would like + action: “to book”; “a party of two” means two diners; “Good night” usually ends the conversation, not begins it.

4 · IMPROVE YOUR ANSWER

I want a table → I’d like to book a table for two, please.

Explore the examples and details below, then try the guided exercises.

Morning, afternoon, evening, night

Good morning before noon; good afternoon after noon; good evening on arrival in the evening. Good night is normally a goodbye. Lunch is often midday; dinner is in the evening.

Party of two

A party of two means two diners, not a celebration. “A table for two” is also natural. “For two people, please.”

Would like / Could

Use: “I’d like…” politely states a wish or makes a request. Affirmative: I’d like a table (noun). I’d like to book a table (to + verb). Negative for a choice: I’d prefer not to sit near the door; I don’t want a table by the kitchen. Question: Would you like a table outside? / Could I book a table for two? Form: could + base verb; would like + noun or to + verb. Why not “I wouldn’t like…”? Grammatically possible, but unnatural for an ordinary restaurant refusal; use “No, thank you” or “I’d rather not.”

Inside, outside and patio

Could we sit inside? Do you have outdoor seating? Could we have a table on the patio? A patio is an outdoor seating area.

Prepositions of time

At 7:30 p.m.; on Friday; in the evening; for two people. “On Friday at seven in the evening.”

Availability and exceptions

Do you have a table? Does the restaurant have patio seating? Is there a table available? After does, use have, not has.

Step 2 · Arrive, sit down and order
Practise the full restaurant journey: host, table, menu, food and drinks.
GRAMMAR · LEARN BEFORE YOU PRACTISE
Present simple, would like and food quantities
1 · WHY / WHEN
Order naturally and say what you do not want
Use “I’d like…” to order politely. Use “I don’t want…”, “without…” or “No…, please” for a negative request. The server may use “Would you like…?” to offer something.
2 · HOW TO BUILD IT
Order: I’d like the steak, please. Negative: No sauce, please / I don’t want any sauce, thank you. Question: Could I have it without sauce? Server: Would you like a drink?
3 · EXCEPTIONS & COMMON MISTAKES
“I would take a steak” is not a normal restaurant order. Say “I’ll have the steak” or “I’d like the steak.” “I wouldn’t like any sauce” is grammatical but sounds unnatural here. “Any” often appears in negatives and questions; “some” often appears in offers and requests.
4 · IMPROVE YOUR ANSWER
Steak, sauce separately → I’ll have the steak. Could I have the sauce on the side, please?
Explore the examples and details below, then try the guided exercises.
The host welcomes you
“Welcome! Do you have a reservation?” → “Yes, a table for two under Valérie.” “Would you prefer inside or outside?” → “Inside, please.”
Ordering with would like
Use: For a polite order or offer, use I’d like + noun: “I’d like the steak, please.” Or I’d like + to + verb: “I’d like to try the soup.” Question from the server: “Would you like any sauce?” Natural negative: “No sauce, please.” / “I don’t want any sauce, thank you.” / “Could I have it without sauce?” Different meaning: “I wouldn’t like that” describes an imagined dislike, not how we usually order. Say “I’ll have the steak”, not “I would take a steak.”
Countable / uncountable
How many burgers? Two burgers. How much water? Some water. Rice, bread and water are usually uncountable; a glass of water and two slices of bread are countable.
Some / any / a / an
I’d like some water. Do you have any vegetarian dishes? I’d like an appetizer. Use a/an for singular countable nouns.
On the side / without
Could I have the dressing on the side? I’d like the burger without onions. Can I substitute fries for salad? Ask; substitutions may cost extra.
Questions about ingredients
Does this dish contain nuts? Is it spicy? Are there any dairy-free options? For allergies, tell the staff clearly and confirm safety.

Step 3 · Handle a problem politely
A missing side dish, a cold meal, or a question about the restrooms.
GRAMMAR · LEARN BEFORE YOU PRACTISE
Polite problems · Present simple and present continuous
1 · WHY / WHEN
Describe the problem, then ask for help
Use present simple for a fact (“The soup is cold”) and present continuous for an action happening around now (“We’re waiting for our drinks”). Then ask politely for a solution.
2 · HOW TO BUILD IT
Fact: My salad is missing. Ongoing: We’re waiting for the salad. Negative: The order isn’t correct. Request: Could you check, please?
3 · EXCEPTIONS & COMMON MISTAKES
“There is” + singular / uncountable; “there are” + plural. “We are wait” ✗ → “We are waiting” ✓. “Could you to check” ✗ → “Could you check” ✓.
4 · IMPROVE YOUR ANSWER
Excuse me, my side salad hasn’t arrived. Could you check, please?
Explore the examples and details below, then try the guided exercises.
There is / There are
Positive: There is a problem. Negative: There isn’t any sauce. Question: Is there another table? Use are for plural: There are two missing drinks.
My meal is cold
Use be + adjective: My food is cold. The order isn’t correct. Is this dish vegetarian?
Present continuous: happening now
am/is/are + verb-ing. “We are waiting for our food.” “The server isn’t coming yet.” “Are you bringing the side salad?” This describes what is happening now, not a regular fact.
Could you / Would it be possible
Could you heat this up, please? Could I have a new plate? Would it be possible to change my order? After could, use base verb.
Restrooms (US)
Excuse me, where are the restrooms? “Bathroom” is also common; “toilet” is less usual as a polite public question in the US.
Keep a friendly tone
“Excuse me, I think there’s a small problem.” → Explain the issue → Make a request → “Thank you for your help.”

Step 4 · Ask for the check and calculate the tip
Learn the American restaurant bill, sales tax and tipping customs.
GRAMMAR · LEARN BEFORE YOU PRACTISE
The check, percentages and money questions
1 · WHY / WHEN
Understand the check before using the calculator
In US restaurants, the menu subtotal, tax, service fees and voluntary tip may be separate. Learn the usual tipping range first, then apply the percentage to the pre-tax subtotal.
2 · HOW TO BUILD IT
Question: Could we have the check? Clarification: Is gratuity already included? Negative: I don’t want to leave a second tip by mistake. Calculation: $50 × 0.20 = $10.
3 · EXCEPTIONS & COMMON MISTAKES
A service fee is not always a gratuity paid to the server. Ask staff whether an automatic charge is gratuity before adding more. Different restaurants have different policies. Card terminals can suggest tips even at optional-tipping counters.
4 · IMPROVE YOUR ANSWER
“Could we have the check, please? Is gratuity included?”
Explore the examples and details below, then try the guided exercises.
Check (US) / bill (UK)
Could we have the check, please? Can we split the check? Is the gratuity included? “Check” is the usual American restaurant word.
How much / how many
How much is the total? How many people are splitting the check? Does the total include tax?
Percentages
“Percent” means “out of 100.” Start with the pre-tax subtotal. Multiply it by 0.18 for 18% or by 0.20 for 20%. Example: on a $50 subtotal, 18% = $9 and 20% = $10. Read 18% as “eighteen percent.” The calculator lets you compare different rates.
Typical customs
Sit-down restaurant with table service: about 18–20% of the pre-tax food-and-drink subtotal is a useful guideline; 20% is a straightforward standard for good service. 15% is a lower-end reference, while 22%+ is generous. Counter service / takeout: a tip is generally optional, even if a screen suggests one. Always check for gratuity already added.
Tax is separate
Why it matters: US restaurant menu prices usually do not include sales tax. The check will show a food-and-drink subtotal, sales tax, perhaps a service fee / gratuity, and a total. A common way to work out a tip is from the pre-tax subtotal, not from the full tax-inclusive total. Use the actual printed tax; do not assume one universal San Francisco percentage.
Negative and clarification
I don’t want to tip twice. The tip isn’t included. Is gratuity already included? Could you explain this service charge?
Tipping is a social convention, not a universal fixed legal requirement. A tip prompt on a payment terminal does not automatically mean you must tip.
🇺🇸 Tipping in San Francisco · What should you actually leave?
In France, service is generally included in the restaurant price. In the United States, a voluntary tip is customary at a full-service sit-down restaurant. It is an important part of local dining etiquette, but there is no one legally required percentage for an ordinary voluntary tip.
15%Lower-end referenceLess than the usual guideline for good full service
18%Common choiceA useful starting point
20%Easy standardTypical for good table service
22%+GenerousFor particularly attentive service
🍔 Fast food or takeout
When you order at a counter and collect your own food, a tip is generally optional. A digital tip screen is a suggestion, not an obligation.
🍽️ Sit-down restaurant
For normal table service, 18–20% of the pre-tax food and drinks is a practical guideline. Use 20% if you prefer an easy number to remember.
🧾 Already included?
Read the check for gratuity included, automatic gratuity, or service charge. These are not always equivalent. Ask: “Is this charge the gratuity?” to avoid tipping twice by mistake.
Example · $50 food subtotal, $4.50 tax
Food and drinks: $50.00 → 20% tip: $50 × 0.20 = $10.00 → Tax: $4.50 → Total: $64.50
The $4.50 tax is an example, not a fixed San Francisco tax rate. Your real check may include other charges.
Useful phrases: “Could we split the check?” · “Can I add the tip by card?” · “Does this total already include gratuity?” · “I’d like an itemized check, please.”

Step 5 · Write a review after dinner
Now that the meal is finished, introduce the past simple to describe what happened.
GRAMMAR · LEARN BEFORE YOU PRACTISE
Past simple · regular and irregular verbs
1 · WHY / WHEN
Tell a completed story about your dinner
Use past simple for a finished experience, often with yesterday, last night or on Friday. Regular verbs usually take -ed; irregular verbs have special past forms.
2 · HOW TO BUILD IT
Statement: We ate at Sotto Mare. Negative: We didn’t order dessert. Question: Did you enjoy it? With be: The staff were friendly.
3 · EXCEPTIONS & COMMON MISTAKES
After did / didn’t, use the base verb: “Did you go?” ✓ / “Did you went?” ✗. The verb be is special: “Was it good?” not “Did it be good?” Pronounce -ed /t/, /d/ or /ɪd/.
4 · IMPROVE YOUR ANSWER
“Last night, we went to a restaurant. The food was delicious, but we waited a long time.”
Explore the examples and details below, then try the guided exercises.
When do we use it?
Use the past simple for a completed action: Yesterday we visited a restaurant. Last night I ordered fish.
Regular verbs: -ed
visit → visited; order → ordered; enjoy → enjoyed; ask → asked. Spelling: like → liked; try → tried; stop → stopped.
Irregular verbs
go → went; eat → ate; have → had; take → took; see → saw; be → was/were. Learn these separately.
Positive form
Subject + past verb: We ate seafood. The service was friendly. I enjoyed the meal.
Negative form
Subject + didn’t + base verb: I didn’t like the dessert. We didn’t wait long. With be: The food wasn’t cold.
Question form
Did + subject + base verb? Did you enjoy the meal? What did you order? With be: Was the service good? Not “Did you enjoyed?”
Past -ed pronunciation
/t/ asked, liked; /d/ played, enjoyed; /ɪd/ wanted, visited. The ending is not always pronounced as an extra syllable.
Review structure
1. Say where you went. 2. Describe the food and service. 3. Mention a positive or problem. 4. Give your recommendation. Connect with and, but, because, so, although.

Describe what you can see and want to visit
1 · WHY / WHEN
Describe a place and make a sightseeing plan
Use there is/are for what exists, can for possible activities, and want to / would like to for your own plans. Connect your reason with because.
2 · HOW TO BUILD IT
Statement: There is a museum near downtown. Negative: There aren’t any ferry departures from this pier. Question: Are there tours? Plan: I’d like to visit Alcatraz.
3 · EXCEPTIONS & COMMON MISTAKES
“There is” is singular; “there are” is plural. After can, use a base verb (“can visit,” not “can to visit”). After want / would like + action, add to (“want to visit”).
4 · IMPROVE YOUR ANSWER
“I’d like to visit the California Academy of Sciences because I love animals and science.”
Explore the examples and details below, then try the guided exercises.
There is / there are
There is a bridge. There are museums. There isn’t a ferry from every pier. Are there guided tours?
Can / could for possibilities
You can visit the waterfront. You can’t drive to Alcatraz. Could we take a ferry? After can/could, use base verb.
Want to / would like to
I want to see Chinatown. I would like to visit the museum. I don’t want to walk uphill. Do you want to visit the bridge?
Comparatives for attractions
The bridge is farther away than the waterfront from downtown. The museum is more suitable than a long outdoor walk on a rainy day.
Giving reasons
I want to visit Alcatraz because I enjoy history. It is raining, so I would prefer a museum.
Opening hours and tickets
What time does the museum open? How much are tickets? Do we need to book ahead?

GRAMMAR · LEARN BEFORE YOU PRACTISE
Directions, prepositions and transport
1 · WHY / WHEN
Understand and give directions step by step
Ask a simple question first (“How do I get to…?”), then choose a transport type, follow direction verbs, and check whether you need to get on or off a vehicle.
2 · HOW TO BUILD IT
Direct: Where is the stop? Polite: Could you tell me where the stop is? Instruction: Turn right, then walk straight ahead. Negative: Don’t cross here.
3 · EXCEPTIONS & COMMON MISTAKES
In an indirect question, do not invert the subject and verb: “Could you tell me where the stop is?” ✓ / “where is the stop” ✗. Say by bus but on foot. Cable cars, streetcars, Muni buses and ferries are different.

Imperatives
Go straight ahead. Turn left. Cross the street. Get off at the next stop. Negative: Don’t cross here.
Location prepositions
The café is next to the station. The museum is across from the park. The stop is between the hotel and the square. Walk along the waterfront.
Transport with by / on
Go by bus, by cable car, by taxi, by streetcar, or on foot. “Take the F-line streetcar.” “Get on” and “get off” a bus.
How long / how far / how much
How long does it take? (time) How far is it? (distance) How much is the fare? (price). “It takes about twenty minutes” is a model, not a verified travel estimate.
Can / should
You can take a taxi. You should check the schedule. You shouldn’t assume all cable cars reach the same destination. After modals use base verb.
Direct vs indirect questions
Direct: Where is the stop? Polite indirect: Could you tell me where the stop is? Do not invert inside the indirect clause.
Transport vocabulary
Cable car: historic cable-driven vehicle on hills. Streetcar: electric rail vehicle, including the F line. Muni bus: city bus. Taxi/cab: paid car ride. Ferry: boat crossing.
