01 · YOUR FIRST 90 DAYS

Do not arrive with answers. Arrive with the right questions.

Your executive value is strongest when experience improves the quality of the conversation instead of replacing discovery.

Votre expérience doit améliorer la qualité des questions et des décisions, pas vous pousser à supposer que vous connaissez déjà le contexte local.

Phased planning with would

Use would + base verb to explain a proposed approach, not a promise: I would listen; I would map stakeholders. Sequence with first, then, after that, finally. Different healthcare systems require local validation.

Clear model: First, I would learn how the product is used. Then, I would meet the stakeholders.

EXECUTIVE PRINCIPLE

Experience gives you hypotheses — not automatic conclusions.

Use phrases such as “One pattern I have seen is…”, “I would want to validate…” and “How does that work here?”. This sounds senior, curious and internationally credible.

A founder asks what you would do first after joining. Which answer is strongest?

Look for the answer that is accurate, specific and appropriately cautious. Model answer: I would first understand the product, the customer journey, the hospital decision process and the assumptions the team still needs to validate.

Open and indirect questions

03 · STAKEHOLDER MAPPING

One hospital. Different definitions of success.

A pilot can fail even when the product works if the wrong people are ignored.

LEARN → NOTICE → PRACTISE → APPLY

Diplomatic stakeholder questions

Use Could you…? / Would you be able to…? for courteous requests. Use Who would approve…? to identify decision authority. Differentiate users, champions, decision-makers and blockers before suggesting a strategy.

Clear model: Could you clarify who would approve the pilot and who would use the product each day?

Check understanding: Choose the appropriately cautious professional statement.

CLINICAL USER

“Does it help my work?”

Workflow, usability, patient care, time.

Ask about friction and daily use.
EXECUTIVE SPONSOR

“Does it solve a strategic problem?”

Quality, capacity, risk, growth, economics.

Clarify the business and care objective.
IT / DATA

“Can we integrate it safely?”

Systems, data, security, support.

Do not promise what specialists must validate.
PROCUREMENT / FINANCE

“Is the value worth the commitment?”

Cost, contract, proof, scalability.

Connect pilot evidence to decision criteria.

A clinician loves the solution, but the pilot is still blocked. What should you check next?

Look for the answer that is accurate, specific and appropriately cautious. Model answer: Which stakeholder owns the next decision, what evidence they need, and which operational or technical concern remains unresolved.

04 · PILOT DESIGN

A pilot is not “try the product.” It is a decision experiment.

Before launch, define the question the pilot must answer, who is involved, what will be measured and what happens next.

LEARN → NOTICE → PRACTISE → APPLY

First conditional for test decisions

If + present simple, will + base verb expresses a plausible future consequence. Use would for hypothetical planning or polite proposals. Set measurable criteria before discussing rollout; do not invent pilot data.

Clear model: If the pilot meets the agreed criteria, we will review the next phase together.

Check understanding: Choose the correct first conditional.

PROBLEM

What are we testing?

One specific clinical or operational problem.

POPULATION

Where and with whom?

Site, team, users, patient group or workflow.

METRIC

What counts as evidence?

Time, quality, adoption, capacity, error, satisfaction.

GOVERNANCE

Who owns what?

Sponsor, clinical lead, project lead, technical support.

RISK

What could block success?

Workflow, data, training, capacity, local requirements.

DECISION

What happens after?

Stop, adapt, extend, scale or gather more evidence.

Which pilot objective is strongest?

Look for the answer that is accurate, specific and appropriately cautious. Model answer: Test whether the solution reduces the target workflow delay while maintaining quality and achieving defined user-adoption criteria.

A founder wants to scale after two enthusiastic users give positive feedback. What is the best response?

Look for the answer that is accurate, specific and appropriately cautious. Model answer: That is encouraging, but I would separate positive feedback from evidence of repeatable adoption. What would we need to validate before scaling?

05 · ADOPTION & CHANGE

Technology works only when people can use it in reality.

Your healthcare-leadership background is especially valuable here: workflow, communication, training, incentives, leadership and follow-up.

LEARN → NOTICE → PRACTISE → APPLY

Evidence before conclusions

Present perfect asks about experience or change up to now: Have clinicians used the tool? Present simple explores routine: How often do teams use it? Past simple asks about a completed event: What happened during onboarding?

Clear model: Have clinicians used the product since training? What feedback have they shared?

Check understanding: Choose the appropriately cautious professional statement.

RED FLAG

“The training was completed, so adoption should be fine.”

Training is only one part of adoption. Workflow, leadership, usability, reinforcement and local ownership still matter.

BETTER

“Training is complete. What do usage patterns tell us now?”

This moves the conversation from activity completed to behavior and operational evidence.

Usage drops after the first two weeks. What is the strongest first response?

Look for the answer that is accurate, specific and appropriately cautious. Model answer: Look at when and where usage drops, speak with users, compare workflow conditions and identify the specific barrier before choosing an intervention.

07 · SENIOR DIPLOMACY & GRAMMAR

Sound confident without sounding absolute.

In a startup, you need to challenge ideas, ask precise questions and express risk without slowing the conversation down.

LEARN → NOTICE → PRACTISE → APPLY

Executive diplomacy: four core structures

1. could / may / might + base verb = possibility (might is natural in both UK and US English). 2. If + present, will + base = realistic condition. 3. I would + base = hypothetical recommendation. 4. Could you tell me + statement-order clause = indirect question. Modals do not take -s or to before the main verb.

Clear model: This could create risk. If we validate adoption, we will have stronger evidence. I would test that assumption. Could you tell me how approval works?

Check understanding: Choose the grammatically correct sentence.

HYPOTHESIS

may / might / could

Express a possibility.

This could indicate…

“This could indicate a workflow issue rather than a training issue.”

CONDITION

if + present, will

Link evidence to action.

If we confirm X, we will…

“If we confirm the bottleneck, we will redesign the pilot step.”

DIPLOMACY

would / I would want to

Challenge without overclaiming.

I would want to validate…

“I would want to validate that assumption with users.”

CLARIFICATION

indirect questions

Ask senior questions naturally.

Could you help me understand…?

“Could you help me understand who owns the decision?”

Choose the most senior way to challenge an assumption.

Look for the answer that is accurate, specific and appropriately cautious. Model answer: I can see the logic. I would still want to validate how that assumption holds in the actual workflow.

Complete: “If the pilot ___ that the workflow improves, we will have stronger evidence for expansion.”

Look for the answer that is accurate, specific and appropriately cautious. Model answer: shows

Choose the most credible risk statement.

Look for the answer that is accurate, specific and appropriately cautious. Model answer: The current pattern may point to an adoption barrier, but I would want to compare usage by team and workflow before concluding.

Micro-corrections

Could you help me ___ how the decision is made?

Verb: understand
After “help me”, use the base form of the verb, without “to”: help me + understand.

If the pilot ___ value, we will discuss the next stage.

Verb: show
This is a first conditional: if + present simple, will + base verb. The subject “the pilot” is singular, so add -s.

I would suggest ___ the workflow assumption first.

Verb: validate
After “suggest”, use the -ing form of the verb: suggest + validating, not suggest to validate.

Founder

Model answer: Because a small amount of mapping early can prevent us from optimizing for the wrong user or discovering a decision-maker too late. Product team

“Users asked for three more features. Should we build them?”

Look for the answer that is accurate, specific and appropriately cautious. Model answer: Before deciding, I would separate the requested feature from the underlying problem. What are users trying to achieve, and is a new feature the only way to solve it?

Hospital partner

“We do not have time for a long pilot.”

Look for the answer that is accurate, specific and appropriately cautious. Model answer: Understood. Could we define the smallest test that still gives both sides enough evidence to make a responsible next decision?

Board / investor

“What would make you hesitate before scaling?”

Look for the answer that is accurate, specific and appropriately cautious. Model answer: I would hesitate if the result depends on one enthusiastic site, if adoption is not repeatable, or if the operating model requires support that cannot scale economically.
