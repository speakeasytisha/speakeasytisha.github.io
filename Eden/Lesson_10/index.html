01 · Flight plan

Your new leadership communication mission

A recent meeting showed something important: when nobody had taken the lead, you created a space for alignment. People came, the meeting was useful, senior management joined, and an important organisational announcement was made. The next step is not to become “more technical than the technical leader”. It is to make roles, decisions, questions and expectations easier for everyone to understand.

FR : Une réunion récente a montré quelque chose d’important : quand personne ne prenait vraiment l’initiative, vous avez créé un espace d’alignement. La prochaine étape n’est pas d’être « plus technique que le leader technique », mais de rendre les rôles, décisions, questions et attentes plus clairs pour tout le monde.

1

Lead without competing

Use the technical leader’s expertise while keeping ownership of coordination, priorities and decisions.

2

Start with the point

Give the headline or question before the history. Add only the context needed to understand it.

3

Adapt the frequency

Adjust speed, detail and evidence to the communication preference in front of you.

4

Make people move

Connect the message to purpose, impact, role and a concrete next step.

The target sentence

“I know the context. I choose the message. I say the point first. Then I give only the evidence needed to move forward.”

02 · Leadership map

Authority does not mean doing every role yourself

In a technical team, leadership can be distributed. One person can carry technical authority while another carries coordination, priorities, stakeholder alignment and people management. Clear ownership is stronger than competing for the same territory.

YOU OWN

Direction & coordination

  • clarify priorities
  • dispatch work
  • make expectations explicit
  • surface risks and blockers
  • close decisions and next steps
TECHNICAL LEAD

Technical depth

  • challenge technical assumptions
  • guide specialist choices
  • validate technical reasoning
  • bring deep expertise
  • explain technical consequences
SHARED

Cooperation zone

  • prepare decisions together
  • translate technical risk into business impact
  • agree who speaks on what
  • avoid contradictory messages
  • protect team credibility
Management principle: Delegating or recognising technical leadership is not a loss of authority. It can strengthen your authority when the boundaries are explicit and you remain accountable for the coordination of the whole.

Say it without sounding defensive

A senior technical colleague starts answering every question in a meeting before you can structure the discussion. What do you say?

Recognition → boundary → cooperation. Example opening: “Your technical input is essential. Let me first frame the decision we need to make…”

Clear model

“Your technical input is essential. Let me first frame the decision we need to make, then I’ll ask you to take us through the technical options.”

Leadership model

“I’d like to separate the decision from the technical analysis. I’ll clarify the objective and the trade-off we need to resolve; then I’d like you to lead the technical recommendation. That way, everyone knows where the decision sits and where the expertise sits.”

03 · Headline first

Your biggest communication shift: stop starting at the beginning

THE OLD PATH

Context → context → more context → detour → question

You try to make sure the other person understands everything before you ask the question. The risk: the listener does not know what to listen for.

→

THE NEW PATH

Headline / question → why it matters → minimum context → next step

The listener knows the destination immediately. Context becomes useful evidence instead of a maze.

04 · Question first

When you need information, make the question visible

You do not have to choose between being direct and giving context. You can do both — but in the right order.

1

Signal

“I have one question about the handover.”

2

Ask

“Who owns the technical validation from Monday?”

3

Add context if needed

“I’m asking because the current reporting line is still unclear.”

4

Close

“Can we confirm the owner before the end of this meeting?”

Instead of…

“So, I wanted to explain because before we had… and then there was… and I’m not sure if…”

Use…

“I need to clarify one point: who makes the final call on this topic?”

If they need context…

“The reason I’m asking is that two people are currently giving different instructions.”

Rewrite the question

Turn this into a direct professional question:

“I wanted to talk to you because we’ve had several meetings and normally the previous manager used to decide, but now since the organisation changed there are different people involved and I’m not sure…”

Your first 12 words should already show the subject or the question.

“I need to clarify one point about the new organisation: who has the final decision on this topic now?”

05 · Convince

Golden Circle — purpose before process

The Golden Circle is a useful presentation framework associated with Simon Sinek: WHY → HOW → WHAT. Use it as a communication tool, not as a rigid rule. Its value for you is simple: when you want people to cooperate, give them a reason to care before you give them the mechanics.

WHY

Purpose / importance

“We need one clear operating rhythm so the team can make decisions without waiting for a hierarchy that is not always available.”

HOW

Method

“We’ll use a short check-in every two weeks, with clear owners and unresolved points.”

WHAT

Action

“For the next meeting, bring one decision, one risk and one request for support.”

20-second convincing message

“We need a predictable way to stay aligned while the organisation is changing. A short biweekly meeting gives us visibility without adding heavy process. For the next month, I suggest we test it with one owner and one decision per topic.”

Purpose: uncertainty creates friction. Method: lightweight rhythm. Action: testable next step. It does not ask people to agree with a long theory.

06 · DISC decoder

Same message. Different frequency.

DISC stands for Dominance, Influence, Steadiness and Conscientiousness. Some providers use red, yellow, green and blue, but colour mappings can vary. Use DISC as a practical hypothesis about communication preference — not as a diagnosis, a box or a reason to stereotype someone.

FR : Le DISC peut aider à adapter votre style de communication, mais il ne faut pas réduire une personne à une couleur. Observez surtout ce dont la personne a besoin : rapidité, relation, stabilité ou précision.

DDominance

Often values: speed, result, autonomy, directness.

Try: lead with outcome, options and decision.

Avoid: a long history before the ask.

IInfluence

Often values: energy, exchange, visibility, enthusiasm.

Try: show the opportunity and invite reaction.

Avoid: drowning the conversation in detail.

SSteadiness

Often values: predictability, cooperation, time to absorb change.

Try: explain what stays stable and how support will work.

Avoid: sudden pressure without reassurance.

CConscientiousness

Often values: accuracy, criteria, logic, evidence.

Try: state the conclusion, then provide the data and criteria.

Avoid: unsupported claims or excessive improvisation.

Your useful stretch: If you naturally combine directness with strong attention to detail, the challenge is not to abandon detail. It is to sequence it: headline first, detail on demand.

07 · Authority language

Firm does not mean aggressive. Cooperative does not mean vague.

Clarify roles

“I’d like to clarify who owns which part before we continue.”

Use expertise

“I’ll rely on you for the technical recommendation; I’ll coordinate the decision and next steps.”

Interrupt constructively

“Let me pause you there for one second so I can make the decision point explicit.”

Disagree

“I see the logic. My concern is the impact on the team, so I’d like us to test one assumption before we decide.”

Close ambiguity

“Before we close, I want to make sure we leave with one owner, one deadline and one escalation point.”

Lead without hierarchy

“Until the reporting line is confirmed, I suggest we use this working rule so the team can keep moving.”

08 · Presence

Make 20 seconds feel intentional

◉

Look before you speak

One second of eye contact before the first sentence makes the opening feel deliberate rather than rushed.

Ⅱ

Pause after the headline

Do not rush to rescue the silence. Let the main point land.

↓

Finish downward

On a statement or recommendation, avoid turning every ending into a question.

□

Reduce movement

Keep gestures purposeful. Physical stillness can make a concise message feel more authoritative.

10 · Fresh start

Set expectations before disagreements set the culture

A new role is easier to stabilise when expectations are explicit. For the first month, a short weekly check-in can focus on one theme at a time instead of trying to solve the whole relationship at once.

WEEK 1

Roles

Who owns what? Where does technical authority sit? What decisions stay with you?

WEEK 2

Communication

How do you want risks, disagreements and urgent questions to be raised?

WEEK 3

Delegation

What can run autonomously? What needs visibility? What requires approval?

WEEK 4

Adjust

What is working? What creates friction? What one behaviour should change next?

Opening for a one-to-one

“I’d like us to use the first month to make our working relationship explicit. My goal is not to change how you use your expertise. I want us to be clear about roles, decisions and how we challenge each other so we can work quickly without creating unnecessary tension.”

Lead the room without overexplaining

Model A

“The priority for me is to keep the team moving while the organisation settles. I’m comfortable with the technical lead owning deep technical coordination; I need to own priorities, interfaces and decision follow-up. For the next month, I suggest a short weekly check-in with clear owners and open points. If we disagree, we surface the disagreement early and decide who needs to arbitrate it. What I need from everyone is transparency and a clear commitment to the agreed next step.”

Model B

“We do not need to wait for a perfect organisation chart to work effectively. What we need now is a clear operating model. I want technical expertise to stay where it is strongest, while I provide the structure around priorities, interfaces, expectations and decisions. For the first month, I propose one short weekly alignment: one risk, one decision and one support request per workstream. When there is disagreement, we make it visible, challenge the facts, and close with an owner and next step. That gives the team both autonomy and clarity.”
