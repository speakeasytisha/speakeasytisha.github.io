03 · Core communication tool

CARE: four moves for useful feedback

Use the structure, not a memorised script.

C

Context

Say when and where. Ground it in something real.

“In the last two reports…”

A

Action

Describe the specific, observable behaviour — not a character judgement.

“…the traceability section was missing.”

R

Result

Explain the concrete impact of that behaviour.

“…which means the auditor cannot follow the evidence.”

E

Expectation

State clearly what you need going forward.

“Going forward, I need that section completed before you send the report.”

CONTEXT → ACTION → RESULT → EXPECTATION

04 · Grammar that supports leadership

First vs second conditional: firm consequences vs softened suggestions

Use the first conditional when a consequence is real and likely — a firm, direct expectation. Use the second conditional when you are offering a hypothetical suggestion or softening advice — useful in coaching, before a pattern is confirmed.

First conditional — real / likely

If + present simple, … will + base verb

Use it for: a real, near-certain consequence. Firm and direct — appropriate once a pattern is confirmed or a standard is non-negotiable.

“If this pattern continues, we will need to revisit your workload.”

“If you miss the deadline again, I will have to escalate it.”

“If the report is late again, the auditor will flag it.”

Second conditional — hypothetical / softened

If + past simple, … would + base verb

Use it for: a suggestion, a hypothesis, or advice that has not (yet) become a firm requirement. Softer, more coaching in tone.

“If you delegated the report earlier, it would help the team plan around it.”

“If I were you, I would be more specific about the deadline up front.”

“If this pattern continued, we would need to revisit your workload.”

First conditional

“If this continues, we will need to revisit your workload.”

Real, confirmed pattern — a firm expectation now.

Second conditional

“If this continued, we would need to revisit your workload.”

Same idea, offered as a hypothesis — a softer, earlier coaching moment.

1. If this pattern continues, we ______ to revisit your workload.

2. If you delegated the report earlier, it ______ the team plan around it.

3. If you miss the deadline again, I ______ escalate it to the programme lead.

05 · Delegation clarity

Hand over a task without handing over confusion

1

Outcome

State the result you need, not the method.

“What I need is a clear, defensible position by Thursday.”

2

Autonomy

Be explicit about how much independence they have.

“How you get there is entirely up to you.”

3

Ownership

Confirm the task and the decisions inside it now belong to them.

“I want you to take full ownership of this report.”

4

Check-in

Agree a specific moment to review progress — not an open-ended “let me know.”

“Let’s set a check-in point for Wednesday.”

07 · Presence in feedback

How you sound decides how it lands

1

Slow down the opening

The first sentence sets the emotional tone. Do not rush into the issue.

2

Name the behaviour, not the person

“The report was late” lands differently from “you are unreliable.”

3

Pause after the expectation

Give the person room to respond instead of filling the silence yourself.

4

End on the relationship

Close with support or a next step, not just the correction.

Listen to the difference

So, um, this isn't a big deal, but the report was kind of late again, and, I don't know, maybe try to be more on time next time?

I want to flag something. PAUSE The traceability section was missing from the last two reports. PAUSE Going forward, I need that section completed before you send it to me.

09 · Managing emotional reactions

When the conversation gets tense

😤

Defensiveness

Acknowledge the valid part first, then return to the specific behaviour.

Say:

“I'm not questioning your experience — I want to flag one specific thing.”

🤐

Silence

Do not fill the gap immediately. Ask an open question and wait.

Say:

“Take a moment. What's your read on this?”

😠

Frustration

Lower your own pace and volume rather than matching theirs.

Say:

“I can see this is frustrating. Let's slow down and look at it together.”

😔

Discouragement

Separate the mistake from their overall value to the team.

Say:

“This doesn't change how much I value your work. Let's fix this one thing.”

Best first move?

An engineer goes quiet and stops responding after you raise a missed deadline.

Best response to defensiveness?

“I've been doing this for fifteen years. I know what I'm doing.”

10 · Recognition and motivation

Make appreciation sound genuine, not automatic

⭐

Be specific

Name the exact contribution, not a generic “good job.”

“Your work on the interface issue closed a risk we'd had open for weeks.”

🎯

Connect to purpose

Show how the work matters to the bigger goal.

“This is exactly the kind of reliability the certification review depends on.”

🔁

Keep momentum

Recognise progress, not only completed results.

“We're not finished, but this is real progress — keep going.”

🤝

Empower

Pair recognition with more trust and independence.

“I'd like you to take the lead on the next one.”

11 · Reporting up

Summarising a team conversation for management

Your manager may ask how a difficult conversation went. Keep it factual, brief and focused on outcome.

A

Answer

Give the headline: resolved, in progress, or needs support.

E

Evidence

One or two concrete facts — not the full emotional story.

N

Next

State the follow-up, owner and check-in date.

12 · CLOE transfer

Spontaneous professional interaction

Situation 1

An engineer on your team asks for feedback on a report they submitted last week. Give it to them, on the spot.

Sure — overall it was solid. The one thing I'd change is the traceability section, which was missing the source references. That means the auditor can't follow the evidence back. If you add that in for next time, it would strengthen the report considerably.

Situation 2

You need to delegate an unfamiliar task to someone more senior than you. Explain how you would approach it.

I would frame it around their expertise rather than hierarchy. I'd be clear about the outcome I need and the deadline, and I'd leave the method entirely up to them — that respects their experience while keeping my coordination role clear.

Situation 3

Describe a time you had to give difficult feedback to someone on your team.

I had a case where one of my engineers had missed several internal deadlines. Instead of addressing each excuse separately, I named the pattern directly and asked what was really behind it. It turned out to be a workload issue, so we adjusted his priorities together, and the deadlines have been met since.
