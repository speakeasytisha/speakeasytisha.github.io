01 · Flight plan

The next step: from executive fluency to leadership legitimacy

You have already practised concise updates, deliberate pauses, eye contact, present perfect status language and spontaneous management Q&A. Today, the pressure changes: the challenge may not be about your project. It may be about whether someone accepts your authority.

FR : Vous avez déjà travaillé les mises à jour concises, les pauses intentionnelles, le regard, le present perfect et les questions imprévues. Aujourd’hui, la pression change : la contestation peut viser non pas le projet, mais votre légitimité en tant que manager.

2

3

Move the discussion back to facts, standards, responsibilities and the next action.

Escalate wisely

Know when resistance becomes repeated undermining, discrimination, harassment or a governance issue.

Leadership principle

You do not need to prove that you “deserve” the role in every conversation.

Your job is to make the decision process, standards, ownership and expectations clear — consistently.

03 · Credibility map

Build authority from four sources — not from age

Q

Qualifications

Use objective requirements, expertise and scope — briefly.

“My background matches the technical and leadership requirements of this role.”

R

Results

Point to what you have delivered, improved or stabilised.

“I have already led cross-functional work through complex validation decisions.”

P

Process

Make decisions transparent: criteria, evidence, owner, deadline.

“Here is the criterion we are using, and here is what would change the decision.”

C

Consistency

Same standard, same follow-through, same respect — especially under pressure.

“We agreed on the owner and date. I am holding us to that commitment.”

Important: unfair age or gender bias is not something you are required to “earn your way out of”. Strong communication helps you lead effectively, but organisational bias is an organisational issue too. If behaviour becomes discriminatory, humiliating, threatening or persistently undermining, document patterns and use the appropriate manager/HR/governance channel.

What creates the strongest authority?

A senior colleague repeatedly questions your competence in front of the team.

Which sentence is strongest?

You need to stop a circular argument.

04 · Diagnose before responding

What kind of resistance is this?

The same sentence can require a very different response depending on what is really happening.

01

Content challenge

“I think your technical assumption is wrong.”

Response:

Invite evidence. Test the reasoning. This is healthy challenge.

02

Priority challenge

“My team cannot deliver that by Friday.”

Response:

Clarify constraints, trade-offs and ownership.

03

Role challenge

“Why are you the one deciding this?”

Response:

Clarify mandate and decision process without giving a biography.

04

Identity/status challenge

“You’re very young to be telling us how to do this.”

Response:

Name or bypass the personal framing, then return to work criteria.

Classify the challenge

“Your evidence does not cover the failure mode we identified last week.”

“No offence, but people normally need another ten years before they get a position like yours.”

“I cannot give you two engineers this week without delaying another critical activity.”

“I used to report this directly to the former manager. I don’t see why I should go through you.”

05 · Core communication tool

CALM: four moves for resistance

Use the structure, not a memorised script.

C

Confirm

Show that you heard the relevant point.

“I understand the concern.”

A

Anchor

Return to the standard, evidence, mandate or objective.

“The criterion we agreed is…”

L

Lead

State the direction, boundary or decision.

“Based on that, we are going to…”

M

Move

Close with ownership, next action or check-back point.

“Please send the evidence by Thursday; we will review it Friday.”

06 · Grammar that supports credibility

Present perfect for accumulated evidence

Use present perfect when the past experience is relevant to your credibility or situation now. Use past simple for a finished, dated event.

Present perfect

have / has + past participle

Use it for: experience up to now, recent progress, repeated achievements, unfinished time periods.

“I have led cross-functional reviews.”

“We have resolved three of the four open points.”

“I have worked with these requirements for several years.”

Present perfect

“I have managed complex interfaces.”

Experience relevant now; no finished date.

Past simple

“I managed the X review last March.”

Finished event at a specific past time.

1. I ______ several cross-functional validation activities, so I know where the main interface risks usually appear.

2. We ______ the supplier review on 12 August.

3. We ______ the decision criteria with the senior engineering team.

4. I ______ responsible for this workstream since the beginning of the phase.

30-second credibility sprint

Complete these in your own words, then say them aloud without adding an apology.

08 · Presence under challenge

What your voice does before your words land

1

Pause before the boundary

Do not rush into a defensive answer. One beat can change the perceived power of the sentence.

2

Shorten the sentence

When challenged personally, fewer words usually sound stronger than a long justification.

3

Lower the ending

A calm falling cadence helps a decision sound complete instead of negotiable.

4

Hold eye contact on the decision line

Look at the person when stating the standard or boundary, then move naturally back to the group.

Listen to the difference

I mean, I know I am younger, but I do have experience, and I really hope we can work together on this.

10 · Interruptions & public testing

Keep the floor without fighting for it

First interruption

“Let me finish the point, and then I will come directly to you.”

Repeated interruption

“I do want your input. I also need to finish the sentence so the team has the full context.”

Public undermining

“If you disagree, challenge the decision with evidence. Let’s not personalise the discussion.”

After the meeting

“I value your expertise. The repeated interruptions made it difficult to lead the discussion. I need you to challenge the content without undermining the meeting process.”

Boundary ladder: do not jump to maximum force immediately

1

Redirect: “I’ll come to you in one moment.”

2

Name the behaviour: “You have interrupted three times. I need to finish the point.”

3

Reset expectation privately: “Challenge the content, not the meeting authority.”

4

Document / escalate: when the pattern continues or crosses policy boundaries.

11 · Cooperation without surrender

Turn experienced sceptics into contributors

Use expertise visibly

Ask senior colleagues for domain knowledge without transferring your decision responsibility.

“You know this system history better than anyone here. What failure mode would you test first?”

Pre-wire difficult decisions

Speak to key experts before the formal meeting when a decision will affect them strongly.

“I want your strongest technical objection before we go into the review.”

Define decision rights

Clarify who advises, who decides and who owns execution.

“I want your recommendation. I will make the final trade-off and remain accountable for it.”

Give credit publicly

Recognise expertise and contribution. Authority is not weakened by giving credit.

“This risk was identified by the systems team, and their analysis changed our approach.”

Oral transfer

Your most sceptical senior colleague is also your strongest technical expert. How do you involve them without letting them become the unofficial manager?

Reveal model

I would make the distinction between expertise and decision responsibility very clear. I would actively use their technical knowledge, especially before important reviews, and I would give them visible credit when their analysis improves the decision. At the same time, I would keep ownership of priorities, trade-offs and final decisions explicit. That gives them a meaningful role without creating a second management line.

12 · When the issue is age or gender bias

Do not absorb a structural problem as a personal communication failure

Low-level assumption

“You look too young to be the manager.”

Possible response: “I get that my profile may not be what you expected. The role has clear requirements, and I meet them. Let’s look at what the team needs from me to succeed.”

Repeated undermining

“Ask one of the senior men to confirm before we follow that decision.”

Possible response: “The decision does not require validation based on age or gender. If you have a technical objection, bring me the evidence and I will review it.”

Disrespect disguised as humour

A repeated joke questions whether a younger woman can manage the team.

Possible response: “I want to be clear that comments about age or gender are not useful to the work. Please stop. If there is a concern about a decision, raise it directly.”

Communication is not the only tool

When behaviour is repeated, discriminatory, harassing, retaliatory or materially obstructs your ability to manage, the right response may include documenting dates/examples, checking company policy, involving your own manager, HR or another appropriate channel. The objective is not to “win” every interaction alone.

14 · CLOE transfer

Spontaneous professional interaction

Situation 1

You have recently become the manager of an experienced team. Explain how you would build trust during your first month.

I would start by listening carefully to the team and understanding what already works well. I have managed cross-functional work before, so I know that credibility comes from both competence and consistency. I would clarify priorities and decision responsibilities early, involve experienced colleagues in technical discussions, and make sure I follow through on what I say.

Situation 2

A colleague openly disagrees with your decision in a meeting. Respond professionally and keep the meeting productive.

I want to hear the objection, but let’s keep it specific. What evidence do you think changes the decision? If there is new information, we will review it. If not, we need to move forward with the agreed plan and confirm the next action.

Situation 3

Describe a professional situation in which you have had to persuade people who did not initially agree with you.

I have had several situations where different technical teams had conflicting priorities. In one case, I first clarified the common objective, then asked each team to explain the risk behind its position. Once the trade-offs were visible, I proposed a solution with clear ownership and follow-up. The disagreement did not disappear immediately, but the teams were able to move forward around a shared decision.

15 · Final mission

Leadership meeting: authority under pressure

Integrate everything: pause, present perfect, CALM, evidence, boundary and next action.

Scenario

You have recently become manager of a senior technical team.

Two experienced colleagues have challenged the new operating method. One says the method is unnecessary. The other says privately that the team is “not ready to be managed by someone so young.” Management asks you to explain the situation, preserve delivery and prevent the conflict from damaging the project.

  1. Give the headline.
  2. Use present perfect to show what has already been done.
  3. Separate technical resistance from legitimacy resistance.
  4. State one boundary.
  5. Explain how you will use senior expertise.
  6. End with a clear next action.
