01 · Flight plan

The management skill behind senior autonomy

When your direct reports are highly experienced technical leaders, your value is not measured by how many instructions you give. Your value is measured by whether everyone understands the outcome, the boundaries, the decision logic and the accountability.

FR : Avec des responsables techniques expérimentés, votre valeur ne vient pas du nombre d’instructions données, mais de la clarté du résultat attendu, des limites, de la logique de décision et des responsabilités.

Leadership principle

03 · Delegation architecture

Delegate outcomes, not methods

The more senior the expert, the more important it is to separate what must be achieved from how the expert achieves it.

1

Outcome

“What I need from you is…”

Define the result, not the procedure.
2

Guardrails

“The non-negotiables are…”

State safety, compliance, cost, timing or governance constraints.
3

Autonomy

“I’ll leave the technical approach to you.”

Make trust explicit.
4

Check-in

“Come back to me if…”

Define when management needs to re-enter.
Micromanaging

“First, run the analysis again. Then compare the two configurations, speak to quality, update the matrix and send me the slides before noon.”

Outcome-based delegation

“I need a recommendation by noon that makes the compliance impact and the main trade-off visible. I’ll leave the analysis route to you. If you find a safety or certification issue, bring me in immediately.”

Which delegation is strongest?

You need an experienced expert to evaluate a design change.

Which sentence protects autonomy?

The expert asks how you want the analysis performed.

Model: delegate a complex task
B2

What I need from you is a clear recommendation on whether we can keep the current design without creating an unacceptable validation risk. The safety and compliance requirements are fixed. I’ll leave the technical analysis to you. Please bring me in if the risk level changes or if the decision could affect the programme milestone.

C1

Own the technical recommendation and the route you use to reach it. The guardrails are straightforward: no compromise on compliance, no hidden residual risk, and any material schedule impact must be surfaced early. I do not need to approve your method. I need visibility if the decision space changes.

05 · Core framework

ASK → TEST → DECIDE → ALIGN

Use this when a senior expert gives a recommendation and you need to lead the decision without pretending to be the technical specialist.

ASK

Get the judgement

“What do you recommend, and why?”

Demandez une recommandation explicite.

TEST

Pressure-test it

“What assumption matters most?”

Testez hypothèses, risques, alternatives et preuves.

DECIDE

Make ownership visible

“Based on that, we will…”

Prenez ou confirmez la décision.

ALIGN

Close the loop

“You own X by Friday. Reopen only if Y changes.”

Clarifiez responsable, date et condition de réouverture.

High-value questions that challenge judgement—not status

Full model: expert recommendation

Expert: “Technically, I think we should keep the current design. Changing it now creates more risk than it removes.”

Your response — B2: “Okay. What is the main technical reason for keeping it? What risk would the change introduce, and is there any condition that would make you reconsider? Based on what you’ve explained, I agree that we should keep the current design. Please document the residual risk and the mitigation plan by Thursday.”

Your response — C1: “Understood. I want to test the recommendation rather than the expertise behind it. Which assumption is doing the most work here, and what evidence would invalidate it? If the residual risk remains inside our acceptance criteria, we will keep the current design. You own the mitigation note and the evidence trace by Thursday; reopen the decision only if that assumption changes.”

06 · Language precision

Choose the level of managerial force

Senior experts notice vague language. Use the modal that matches the real level of obligation.

OPEN

Could / Would

Exploration, invitation, professional challenge.

“Could you walk me through the assumption?”

EXPECT

I need / I expect

Clear managerial requirement without unnecessary aggression.

“I need the risk to be explicit before we close.”

BOUNDARY

Must / cannot

Non-negotiable compliance, safety or governance boundary.

“We cannot accept an undocumented residual risk.”

07 · Accountability without micromanagement

Control the interfaces, not every action

Agree the deliverable

What exactly will exist at the end?

Agree the owner

Who is accountable for producing it?

Agree the deadline

When does it become late—not “soon”?

Agree the escalation trigger

What change requires management attention?

08 · Challenge lab

Challenge the recommendation without challenging the expert

Use professional curiosity first. Escalate the force only when the risk or governance requires it.

Assumption

“That has always been a safe assumption.”

Your job: test whether it is still safe today.

B2: “What has changed since we first made that assumption?”
C1: “What evidence tells us the assumption still holds under the current configuration?”
Trade-off

“The technically clean solution will add six weeks.”

Your job: surface the programme consequence without dictating the technical answer.

B2: “What do we gain technically, and what do we lose on the programme?”
C1: “What risk are we buying down with those six weeks, and is that reduction proportionate to the schedule cost?”
Confidence

“I’m confident this will work.”

Your job: turn confidence into observable evidence.

B2: “What evidence gives you the most confidence?”
C1: “Which piece of evidence should the decision depend on, and what would disconfirm it?”
Boundary

“We can document the risk later.”

Your job: state the governance boundary.

B2: “We cannot close the decision before the residual risk is documented.”
C1: “The technical route is yours; traceability is not optional. We close only when the residual risk is documented and owned.”

10 · Meeting leadership

When two experts disagree

Your job is not to become the third technical voice. Your job is to make the disagreement decidable.

1

State the decision

“The decision we need today is…”

2

Separate criteria

“Let’s separate safety, performance and schedule.”

3

Hear both recommendations

Same questions. Same evidence standard.

4

Close

Decision, owner, date, reopening condition.

Live meeting

Expert A wants to keep the current design to protect the milestone. Expert B wants a redesign to reduce long-term technical risk. Both are senior, credible and strongly opposed.

02:00
Reveal meeting model
B2

The decision we need is whether the technical risk justifies changing the design at this stage. Let’s separate the criteria: safety and compliance first, then technical performance, then schedule. I want each of you to give your recommendation, the strongest evidence and the main downside of your own option. Once we have that, I’ll confirm the decision and the follow-up actions.

C1

We are not deciding whose expertise is stronger; we are deciding which risk profile the programme should accept. We will use the same criteria for both options: compliance, residual technical risk, reversibility and schedule exposure. I want each recommendation stated with the evidence that supports it and the condition that would make you change your view. I will close with the decision owner, action and reopening trigger.

11 · Rewrite the manager

Remove control, vagueness and over-explanation

Rewrite each sentence so that it sounds like leadership between experienced professionals.

“I need you to do it exactly this way because I think it will be safer.”

B2: “The safety requirement is the priority. I’ll leave the technical method to you, but I need the evidence to show that the requirement is protected.”
C1: “The non-negotiable is the safety outcome, not my preferred method. Use your judgement on the route; make the evidence traceable.”

“Can you maybe keep me updated from time to time?”

B2: “Please update me Thursday with the recommendation and any issue affecting the milestone.”
C1: “I don’t need continuous updates. Bring me in Thursday, or earlier if the risk, scope or milestone changes.”

“I know you have more experience than me, but I hope you can understand why I need to check everything.”

B2: “I value your experience. I don’t need to check every technical step; I do need visibility on the risks, decisions and commitments.”
C1: “Your technical ownership is clear. My accountability is to keep the decision, interfaces and risk exposure visible.”

13 · CLOE transfer

Explain your management approach clearly

Professional oral task: answer naturally for 60–90 seconds with a clear structure and concrete example.

CLOE-style prompt

“How do you manage people who are more experienced than you in a particular technical area?”

01:30
Reveal CLOE model answers
B2

When someone has more technical experience than I do, I see that as an advantage for the team. I make the expected outcome and the constraints clear, and I give the person autonomy on the technical approach. My role is to ask questions about risks, assumptions and impact, and to make sure decisions are aligned with the wider project. For example, if an expert recommends keeping a design, I will ask what evidence supports the recommendation and what could change it. Then we agree on the decision, the owner and the next milestone.

C1

I do not equate management with having the deepest expertise in every domain. With senior specialists, I define the decision space rather than the method: outcome, non-negotiables, interfaces and escalation triggers. I expect the expert to own the technical judgement, and I pressure-test that judgement through assumptions, evidence and trade-offs. That preserves autonomy while keeping management accountability explicit. The result is usually a stronger decision because the expertise remains intact without becoming disconnected from programme priorities.

14 · Final mission

Leadership meeting: autonomy under pressure

Integrate delegation, challenge, decision ownership, accountability and executive compression.

Scenario

You manage three senior technical project leaders.

One wants detailed instructions before acting. One refuses regular check-ins because “senior people should be trusted.” The third has proposed a technically strong solution that creates a serious programme impact. Senior management wants your recommendation and your plan for keeping the team accountable without slowing them down.

  1. State your management principle.
  2. Delegate the outcomes and boundaries.
  3. Explain how you will challenge expert recommendations.
  4. Address the missed visibility / check-in issue.
  5. Make one decision.
  6. Close with owner, deadline and escalation trigger.

Reveal final mission models after your attempt
B2 · Strong professional

My principle is to give senior people autonomy on the technical method while being very clear about the outcome, the constraints and the points where management needs visibility. I would not give detailed instructions unless they are necessary. I would agree the deliverable, owner, deadline and escalation trigger. When an expert makes a recommendation, I will ask about the evidence, the main assumption and the trade-offs before making the wider project decision. For the person who refuses check-ins, I would explain that trust is not the issue: two-way visibility is part of accountability. For the technically strong solution with a serious programme impact, I would ask for the risk reduction to be quantified before deciding. We will review the recommendation Friday, with the expert owning the evidence and me owning the final programme trade-off.

C1 · Executive

I would manage senior experts through decision architecture rather than task control. Each person gets clear ownership of the outcome, explicit non-negotiables and freedom on the technical route. My intervention points are defined in advance: material change in risk, scope, schedule or governance. I pressure-test recommendations through evidence, assumptions, reversibility and programme consequences, then make the management trade-off explicit. Refusing all check-ins is not autonomy; it removes the visibility required for accountability. In this case, the expert keeps technical ownership, but I will set the review cadence until delivery becomes predictable. For the technically superior option with major programme impact, we will quantify the risk reduction against the schedule exposure and make a deliberate decision. The expert owns the technical case by Friday; I own the final programme decision and the communication upward.
