2 · Adaptation

Your colours: DISC as a dial, not a label

15 min

You described yourself as a mix of red and blue: direct and results-driven, with a strong detail-and-accuracy side for the technical work. You love working with blue people. That is your comfort zone — and it tells you exactly where communication will cost you more effort: with yellow and green listeners.

Red — results

Needs: the headline, options, a decision, a deadline.

Avoid: long history before the point.

You: natural. Just don’t forget the “why”.

Yellow — energy

Needs: the vision, the people, the story, enthusiasm.

Avoid: tables of data and dry process.

You: effort zone. Start with the why and a story.

Green — stability

Needs: reassurance, what stays the same, a clear transition, time.

Avoid: surprise pressure and abrupt tone.

You: effort zone. Slow down; name what does not change.

Blue — accuracy

Needs: facts, criteria, logic, assumptions, time to check.

Avoid: asking for trust without evidence.

You: comfort zone. Risk: too much detail for others.

The dial question: not “What colour is this person?” but “What does this person need from me right now — speed, energy, stability or evidence?” The same person can need different things on different days.

One message, four colours — listen and compare

The news: a qualification test has slipped by two weeks because a test bench is unavailable.

To a red N+2

Short version: the qualification test moves by two weeks — the bench isn’t available. There’s no impact on the milestone if we get the backup bench by Friday. I need your approval to request it today.

To a yellow programme manager

I want to tell you where we are, because the team has been doing great work and I don’t want this to overshadow it. We’ve hit a bump — the test bench isn’t available, so qualification moves by two weeks. The good news is we have a plan to get back on track together, and I’d love your support to make it happen.

To a green team member

I want to let you know about a change before you hear it elsewhere. The qualification test is moving by two weeks because the bench isn’t available. Your preparation work stays exactly the same, and nothing changes for your deadlines this week. I’ll confirm the new date on Friday, and if you have any concerns, come and see me.

To a blue technical lead

The qualification test moves by two weeks. The root cause is bench availability, not a technical issue — the configuration is frozen and unchanged. The backup bench has the same calibration standard; I’ve attached the comparison. Could you check whether any test case depends on the specific bench setup before Friday?

3 · Executive update

The programme update — SRIRD, answer first

15 min

You used SRIRD in Lesson 3 to turn technical detail into a strategic message. Now combine it with answer first: in a programme review, the listener should know within ten seconds whether they need to make a decision.

Headline

One sentence first

“One item needs a decision today: …”

S · R

Situation + Risk

What happened, factually. What could go wrong if nothing changes.

I · R

Impact + Recommendation

Cost, schedule, safety, certification. What you propose and why.

D

Decision

The exact decision you need, from whom, by when.

Build your 90-second update

During an integration test, an unexpected behaviour appeared on a subsystem interface. The analysis will take ten days. The programme wants to keep the date for the next test phase.

Your update will appear here.

One item needs a decision today: whether we start the next test phase on schedule or wait ten days. During integration testing we observed an unexpected behaviour on a subsystem interface. We don’t yet know the cause. If we move on without the analysis, we risk running the next phase on a configuration we may have to change — and repeating several weeks of tests. My recommendation is a split approach: we start the test cases that don’t involve this interface on schedule, and hold the others for ten days until the analysis is complete. That protects most of the schedule without taking a blind risk. What I need from you is agreement on that split today, so the team can re-plan tomorrow.

4 · Leadership

Circle of Safety: your fresh-start meeting

20 min
OUTSIDE PRESSURE
THE CIRCLE
TEAM

In Leaders Eat Last, Simon Sinek describes the Circle of Safety: when a leader makes people feel safe inside the team, they stop protecting themselves from each other and start working together against the pressures outside.

In V&V this is not a soft idea. If people don’t feel safe to say “this result looks wrong” or “I don’t understand”, problems are discovered late — and that is the most expensive place to find them.

Leaders eat last.

— Simon Sinek’s image for leadership: the leader takes care of the team first

Your plan, from our first session

You talked about a fresh start: stating your expectations, agreeing how the team will handle disagreements, and checking in — one topic per week during the first month. This is the meeting where the team first hears you as their leader. It is also your first real long-form talk: about five minutes.

Why

Why this team matters, and why you want to start this way.

2

Expectations

What you expect from them — and what they can expect from you.

3

Disagreement

4

Rhythm

Weekly check-in, one topic per week for the first month. Then a question to the team.

Build your fresh-start talk

Your fresh-start talk will appear here.
Model (about 3 minutes — expand each part with your own examples to reach 5)

I want to start with why I asked for this time together. When the organisation around us is changing, the easiest thing is to keep our heads down and wait. I don’t want us to wait. The work we do is what allows everyone else to trust this aircraft — so we deserve to work in a team where people feel safe to speak up. That’s what I want to build with you.

So let me be clear about expectations — in both directions. What I’ll ask of you is three things: raise problems early, even when they are only a feeling; bring facts when we disagree; and keep each other informed across workstreams. What you can expect from me: I will protect your time for testing, I will tell you what I know as soon as I know it, and I will back you when you raise a problem.

On roles: X leads on technical decisions — that expertise is one of our biggest strengths and I want us to use it fully. My role is priorities, coordination and making sure decisions are followed through.

We will disagree — and that’s healthy. I’d just like us to do it in the room, with facts, and then commit together once we decide. Disagreement in the corridor is where teams lose trust.

For the first month, we’ll have a short weekly check-in, one topic each week: first our priorities, then how we share information, then how we escalate, and finally what’s working and what isn’t.

Before we finish, I’d like to hear from you: what is one thing that would make this team easier to work in?

5 · Roles

The technical leader: agree the split — then check back

10 min

You told me you are comfortable with the technical leader taking the technical roles. The risk is not conflict — it is ambiguity. A short one-to-one conversation, closed with a check-back, prevents months of “I thought you were doing that”.

1

Recognise

“Your technical expertise is one of the team’s biggest strengths.”

2

Propose the split

“I suggest you own… and I own…”

3

Name the interface

“When a technical choice changes another team’s priority, we…”

4

Check back

“So, to make sure we agree: … Is that right?”

Close the conversation with a check-back

A check-back has four parts: what you understood, who owns what, a request for correction, and the next step with timing.

So, to make sure we’re aligned: you own the technical recommendations and the arbitration on technical choices. I own priorities, coordination between workstreams, and follow-up on decisions. When a technical choice changes another team’s priority, we bring it to the weekly alignment before closing it. Is that right? If so, I’ll share this with the team at Monday’s check-in.

6 · Feedback

SBI-I: feedback without accusation

10 min

When a behaviour needs to change, stay with facts. Situation → Behaviour (observable) → Impact → Inquiry (ask for their view). It is direct — which suits your red side — and cooperative, which protects the relationship.

In yesterday’s team meeting, a strong technical colleague answered three questions in detail before you could frame the decision the team needed to make. You want cooperation, not a power struggle.

Your SBI-I feedback will appear here.

Can I share something from yesterday’s meeting? When the questions came in, you went straight into the detailed technical answers before I had framed the decision we needed to make. The input was really useful — but the team followed the detail, and we left without a clear decision. How did you see it? And how could we split it next time — I frame, you bring the expertise?

45

I understand why we’re looking for time — the delay upstream is real. But cutting verification from four weeks to two would mean skipping around forty percent of the planned test cases. My concern is that we’d be discovering problems during certification instead of now, when they are much cheaper to fix. What I suggest is that we keep the full window for the safety-critical cases and run the lower-risk ones in parallel with the next phase. That way we recover most of the time and still go into certification with evidence we can defend.

8 · Language

Assertive English — not over-soft English

5 min

French speakers often transfer politeness strategies that sound much weaker in English: “maybe we could perhaps think about…”. Polite English is short, clear and warm. Soften the relationship, not the message.

Over-soft

“I was just wondering if maybe it would be possible to perhaps have a small look at the planning?”

→
Assertive + warm

“Could we review the planning together this week? I have a concern about the verification window.”
