2 · Your model

Why Simon Sinek captivates

10 min

Simon Sinek’s TED talk How Great Leaders Inspire Action is one of the most watched talks ever, and he now hosts a podcast, A Bit of Optimism. He is not a technical expert on flight, Apple or civil rights — yet he talks about all three and people listen. Here are five habits you can copy.

Habit 1

He starts with a belief

Not “Today I’ll present…”, but a big question or conviction the audience instantly cares about.

Habit 2

He repeats one line

A short signature sentence comes back again and again, so you remember it on the way home.

Habit 3

He tells stories

Ideas arrive inside stories — the Wright brothers, Apple, Martin Luther King — never as bullet points.

Habit 4

He is calm

Simple words, a steady pace, real pauses. He sounds like he is thinking with you, not performing at you.

Habit 5

He draws the idea

He famously draws three circles on a flip chart. One simple image anchors a whole talk. You will draw yours in the next section.

People don’t buy what you do; they buy why you do it.

— Simon Sinek, the signature line he repeats throughout his TED talk

3 · Technique

The Golden Circle — and the Wright brothers

15 min
WHAT
HOW
WHY

Most people communicate from the outside in: what they do, then how, and rarely why. Inspiring communicators go from the inside out.

WHY — the purpose, cause or belief. Why should anyone care?
HOW — the approach or values that make you different.
WHAT — the tangible result: the product, the test, the decision, the action.

The story Sinek tells — and it is an aerospace story

At the start of the twentieth century, Samuel Pierpont Langley had everything needed to build the first powered aeroplane: government money, a prestigious position and the best-known minds of his time. Two brothers in Dayton, Ohio, had none of that. They paid for their experiments with the money from their bicycle shop. But the Wright brothers and their small team were driven by a belief — they knew why they were doing it. On the seventeenth of December, 1903, they flew. Sinek’s point is that Langley was chasing the result, while the Wrights were chasing a cause — and a cause is what keeps a team going through every failed test.

FR : Langley avait l’argent, le statut et les meilleurs experts ; les frères Wright finançaient leurs essais avec leur magasin de vélos. Mais ils savaient pourquoi ils le faisaient. Selon Sinek, c’est la cause, et non le résultat, qui fait tenir une équipe à travers les échecs.

Discuss (2 minutes, speaking): Which team in your world is a “Wright brothers” team? What is their why?

4 · Technique

Answer first: Answer → Map → Depth → Return

15 min

You described it yourself: someone asks a question, you think “wait, they won’t understand without the context”, you start with the history — and you feel you have said too much, and not in link with the question. The fix is not less knowledge. It is a different order.

When the thread gets lost

Context → context → context → point?

The listener receives history before knowing what problem the history is supposed to explain.

→
Speaker move

Point → map → only relevant depth → return

The listener knows the destination, so every detail you add has a place to land.

1

Answer

“My question is…” · “The short answer is…” · “My recommendation is…”

2

Map

“There are two reasons.” · “Let me give you the context in two parts.”

3

Depth

Only the facts that support the answer. The rest is available on request.

4

Return

“So, coming back to my question…” · “That’s why I recommend…”

Sinek connection: this is the Golden Circle for a single question. Your answer is the centre. The context is only there to support it.

5 · Delivery

Your voice: pause, stress, land

15 min

A podcast has no slides. Everything the listener feels comes through your voice. Great hosts use four tools constantly — and they are all learnable.

Pause

Pause

After your key sentence, stop for two seconds. Silence tells the listener: this mattered. A pause also replaces “euh”.

Stress

Stress one word

In English, meaning lives in stress. “We proved it works” ≠ “We proved it works”.

Down

Land it — falling tone

End statements with your voice going down. Rising at the end sounds like a question — and like you are asking permission.

Slow

Change the pace

Speed up through context, slow down for the point. Variation is what keeps an ear awake for an hour.

6 · Presence

Body language in the first 20 seconds

5 min

Your notes from our first session: work hard on message and body language, even in 20 seconds. People decide whether to listen before you reach your second sentence. Four anchors — in the room and on camera.

🦶 Ground

Room: both feet flat, weight even. No swaying.
Camera: sit back, shoulders level, screen at eye height.

👀 Land each sentence

Room: finish a sentence on one person’s eyes, then move.
Camera: look at the lens for your first and last sentence.

✋ Open hands

Hands visible, at waist height, palms slightly open. Sinek uses his hands to “draw” his ideas in the air.

🌬️ Breathe, then speak

One breath before your first word. Starting on an out-breath makes your first sentence calmer and lower.

8 · Fluency drill

Just a Minute

10 min

A classic BBC Radio 4 game and a favourite training tool for broadcasters. Speak for 60 seconds on a topic without hesitation, repetition or deviation. It trains exactly what long-form speaking needs: keep going, keep varied, keep on topic.

No hesitation

No “euh”, no long silence while searching. A deliberate pause is allowed — a lost pause is not.

No repetition

Don’t repeat the same key word or phrase. This forces you to find synonyms and new angles.

No deviation

Stay on the topic. If you drift, use a transition to come back: “But the real point is…”
