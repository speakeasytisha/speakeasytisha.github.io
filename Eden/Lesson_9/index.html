02 · France workplace lens

How the French professional context may influence communication

These are broad professional tendencies, not rigid rules about every French person or every company. Use them as intelligent hypotheses, not stereotypes.

Important reminder Interpret first, react second. A direct challenge, a long explanation or a strong debate does not automatically mean hostility.
Hierarchy

Role and authority can matter more

In many French workplaces, hierarchy and role legitimacy may be more visible than in some Anglo-Saxon environments.

Dans de nombreux environnements professionnels français, la hiérarchie et la légitimité du rôle peuvent être plus visibles.
Implication for you: challenge the information gap, the process or the risk — not the person’s status.
Debate

Strong challenge is not always personal

A forceful objection may simply be a way of testing the reasoning, especially in technical and analytical environments.

Un désaccord appuyé peut simplement servir à tester le raisonnement, surtout dans un environnement technique et analytique.
Implication for you: ask for the evidence or logic before interpreting the exchange as personal.
Precision

Clear logic builds credibility

Vague reassurance is often less convincing than a precise explanation of responsibility, risk and next step.

Un discours vague rassure souvent moins qu’une explication précise des responsabilités, des risques et de la suite.
Implication for you: structure your messages around fact → risk → need → next step.
Context

Some people explain the context before the answer

Long explanations can reflect expertise, principles-first reasoning or a desire to transmit the full logic behind a process.

Les longues explications peuvent refléter l’expertise, un raisonnement fondé sur les principes ou le désir de transmettre toute la logique d’un processus.
Implication for you: acknowledge the context, then create a boundary around what you need right now.
Meetings

Meetings may explore ideas before deciding

People may debate extensively, while the actual decision may come later or at another level.

Les réunions peuvent servir à explorer et tester les idées avant qu’une décision soit réellement formalisée.
Implication for you: clarify whether the group is exploring, recommending or deciding.
Fair process

Explicit working rules reduce tension

Clear rules about how information is shared and how disagreements are handled create safety and accountability.

Des règles de travail explicites sur le partage d’information et la gestion des désaccords créent de la sécurité et de la responsabilité.
Implication for you: your authority also comes from making the working process explicit.

Interpretation check

Choose the strongest interpretation.

Evidence base for this cultural lens

The cultural guidance in this lesson is informed by cross-cultural business references such as INSEAD’s work on national communication patterns, Cultural Atlas, and fair-process leadership principles. The aim is not to stereotype, but to help you interpret interactions more intelligently.

  • Hierarchy and role legitimacy may matter more.
  • Analytical debate can be normal and professionally acceptable.
  • Context and logic may come before the direct answer.
  • Explicit communication rules improve cooperation and fairness.

The goal is not to memorise a perfect script. The goal is to understand the move well enough to reproduce it naturally.

01

Communication skill

Separate the person from the communication problem

A difficult person can make you think in labels: controlling, arrogant, defensive or territorial. Those labels may express how the interaction feels, but they do not tell you what to do next. A manager needs to identify the observable communication problem that can be acted on.

Personal interpretation

“She does not want to help me.”

“He has an ego problem.”

“They do not respect me.”

Manageable communication fact

“I still do not have the specific information I need.”

“He is dismissing colleagues instead of challenging the idea.”

“The decision criteria have not been made explicit.”

Why this works: you can respond to an observable fact without proving another person’s intentions. That keeps the conversation professional and reduces the chance of an unnecessary power struggle.

Trainer prompt: Turn the interpretation into a fact you can manage.

Instead of: “My boss is withholding information.”

Say internally: “I still need the current reference, decision criteria and open risks before I take ownership.”

02

Communication skill

Know the objective before you start speaking

Difficult conversations become long when you try to solve the relationship, the history, the technical question, the emotional tension and the future plan at the same time. Decide what you need from this exchange.

Information

“I need the current reference document.”

Decision

“We need to decide which option we are using.”

Boundary

“I need disagreement to remain respectful.”

Ownership

“We need to agree what transfers to me now.”

Feedback

“I need a specific behaviour to change.”

Why this works: once the objective is clear, you can remove words that do not help you achieve it. Concision becomes a management tool, not just an English-language exercise.
Before you speak, complete:

“At the end of this conversation, I need ______________________________.”

03

Communication skill

Acknowledge without surrendering the frame

Acknowledging somebody does not mean agreeing with them. It shows that you heard the concern, expertise or context before you redirect the conversation. This is one of the safest ways to remain firm without sounding combative.

1 · Acknowledge

“I see why that history matters.”

“I want to use your experience here.”

2 · Pivot

“For the handover today…”

“For this decision…”

3 · Direct

“What I need to confirm is…”

“Which evidence should drive the decision?”

Why this works in your context: it preserves respect for seniority and technical expertise while making it clear that the conversation still needs a usable outcome.

Situation: “We have always done it this way.”

Acknowledge: “I understand that this method has worked for the team.”

Pivot: “Given the current requirements…”

Direct: “What are the constraints we must preserve if we improve the process?”

You have not accepted the old method as permanent, but you have not attacked it either.

04

Communication skill

Replace self-defence with inquiry

When somebody questions your competence, defending your CV often makes the exchange more personal. With technical experts, a stronger management move is to return the discussion to the work.

Self-defence

“I have managed teams before and I know I can do this.”

The discussion becomes about whether you are qualified.
Managerial inquiry

“I want the strongest technical input in the room. What criteria should drive this decision?”

The discussion becomes about evidence and decision quality.
Why this works: inquiry is not weakness. You are directing the expert to contribute useful evidence while keeping responsibility for the decision process.

Simple: “What is the main technical risk?”

Stronger: “What evidence supports that conclusion?”

Managerial: “Which technical criteria should carry the most weight in this decision, and why?”

05

Communication skill

Interrupt professionally: protect the purpose, not your impatience

You will sometimes need to interrupt. Attach the interruption to a professional purpose: time, decision quality, inclusion, risk or the unanswered question.

Soft

“Can I pause you for a second to check one point?”

Managerial

“I’m going to pause you there so we can protect the decision time.”

Firm

“I’ve understood the position. I need us to move to the decision now.”

Why this works: the interruption is justified by the needs of the work, not by a personal judgement such as “you talk too much.”
Build your interruption:

“I’m going to pause you there because __________________. What I need now is __________________.”

06

Communication skill

Set a behavioural boundary without attacking personality

“You are arrogant” is a judgement. “You dismissed a colleague’s contribution” is an observable behaviour. Strong managerial feedback names the behaviour, defines the limit and redirects to the expected behaviour.

ALLOW+LIMIT+EXPECTED BEHAVIOUR

ALLOW “Strong disagreement is fine.”

LIMIT “Dismissing a colleague is not.”

EXPECTED BEHAVIOUR “Challenge the technical point and explain the evidence.”

Why this works: it protects robust debate while making the behavioural expectation unmistakable.
07

Communication skill

Close every difficult conversation

A conversation is not finished because everybody has spoken. It is finished when everybody knows what happens next. Closure is a major signal of managerial communication.

Decision

What did we decide?

Owner

Who does what?

Deadline

By when?

Review

When do we check back?

“So the decision is to run the additional test.”

“You’ll own the technical analysis; I’ll manage the stakeholder update.”

“I need the result by Wednesday, and we’ll review the open point Thursday morning.”

Why this works: it reduces ambiguity and prevents the same difficult discussion from restarting because people left with different interpretations.

Guided checkpoint before the scenarios

Identify the communication move before you role-play

Boss is over-explaining→ Acknowledge + narrow + precise question
You have not received the information→ Status + need + risk + next step
Expert challenges your credibility→ Do not defend + ask for evidence + define role
Colleague is dismissive→ Behavioural boundary + redirect to technical point
Meeting is going in circles→ Interrupt for purpose + state decision rule + close
Trainer note: Do not move into free role-play until you can explain why each communication move works. Understanding first; fluency second.

04 · Your communication system

FRAME → FOCUS → EVIDENCE → CLOSE

This is your repeatable system. It works with your boss, with strong experts and with a potentially difficult new team member.

F

FRAME

Say what this part of the conversation needs to achieve.

“I’d like to separate the background from what I need to take over now.”
F

FOCUS

Narrow the discussion to the current responsibility, question or risk.

“For today, I need to focus on the current validation process.”
E

EVIDENCE

Move from status, opinion or emotion to facts, constraints or decision criteria.

“What evidence or constraint should drive that decision?”
C

CLOSE

End the discussion with clarity: decision, owner, next step or review point.

“So I’ll take X, you’ll send Y, and we’ll review the open point on Thursday.”

Why this system works

  • FRAME protects the relationship because you are defining the task, not attacking the person.
  • FOCUS lets you interrupt a long explanation without saying it is useless.
  • EVIDENCE is especially strong with technical experts because it redirects the conversation toward reasoning and facts.
  • CLOSE prevents long discussions from ending in shared confusion.

05 · Situation 1

Your future boss gives you the history instead of the answer

A · Fact

What may be happening?

The long explanation may reflect expertise, habit, principles-first reasoning, or difficulty letting go of the role. You do not need to diagnose the person before responding effectively.

B · Risk

Two weak reactions

  • Passive: you listen for twenty minutes and still leave without the answer.
  • Confrontational: “Can you just answer the question?” makes the context sound worthless and can trigger a status reaction.
C · Strategy

VALUE → BOUNDARY → QUESTION

  1. Acknowledge the context or expertise.
  2. State what you need for the immediate task.
  3. Ask one precise operational question.
D · Why it works

You protect both expertise and efficiency

You are not rejecting the explanation. You are showing respect while creating a clear boundary around what you need now.

Annotated model

VALUE “That background helps me understand why the process evolved.”

→ This validates expertise rather than dismissing it.

BOUNDARY “For the meeting this afternoon, I need to isolate the current operational point.”

→ This makes the narrowing legitimate and task-based.

QUESTION “Who currently has final validation authority?”

→ The question is specific enough to produce a useful answer.

That background helps me understand why the process evolved. For the meeting this afternoon, I need to isolate the current operational point. Who currently has final validation authority?

Live scenario

Your boss has been giving the background for four minutes. You need one clear answer before your next meeting.

06 · Situation 2

You are expected to know something you have not been shown

France lens When hierarchy matters, “You never told me” can sound like an accusation. A stronger response names the information gap and connects it to responsibility and risk.
A · Fact

A knowledge gap is not incompetence

If the responsibility has not yet transferred and the information has not yet been shared, the professional issue is a transition gap.

B · Risk

Do not compensate by pretending

In technical environments, acting as if you know can create more risk than calmly naming the gap.

C · Strategy

STATUS → NEED → RISK → NEXT STEP

Describe what has not transferred, state what you need, explain why it matters, and show what happens once you have it.

D · Why it works

You sound responsible, not defensive

The conversation shifts from blame to safe ownership.

Annotated model

STATUS “I haven’t taken over that part of the process yet.”

NEED “Before I do, I need the current reference and the decision criteria.”

RISK “I don’t want to create risk by working from an assumption.”

NEXT STEP “Once I have those two elements, I can move independently.”

Two-level models

Level 1: “I haven’t been briefed on that part yet. I need the current reference before I take it over.”

Level 2: “I’m ready to take ownership, but I don’t want to create risk by filling the remaining gaps with assumptions. I need the current reference, the decision criteria and any unresolved points. Once I have those, I can move independently.”

07 · Situation 3

Your boss is having difficulty letting go of the role

A · Fact

Do not fight for symbolic authority

The useful question is not “Who is in charge?” but “What transfers, when, and with what information?”

B · Strategy

EXPERTISE → TRANSITION → OWNERSHIP → REVIEW

Show respect for the current role-holder’s experience, then convert the transition into a practical structure.

C · Why it works

You transform tension into process

This protects the relationship while making your future accountability concrete.

Model language

“I want to use your experience during the transition and make sure I take over the role without losing important context. Could we separate the responsibilities into three groups: what stays with you for now, what I take over immediately, and what transfers once I have the necessary information? That would give us both a clear transition path.”

08 · Situation 4

Managing brilliant technical experts who challenge everything

You do not need to out-expert every expert. You need to make the expertise usable for the team.

France lens Strong debate can be normal and even valued. Your task is to preserve intellectual challenge while preventing status competition, personal dismissal or endless discussion.
1

Separate the issue

Disagreement is not the same as disrespect.

2

Ask for evidence

Move from “who knows more” to “what supports this conclusion”.

3

Define the decision rule

Experts can debate forever if nobody knows how the choice will be made.

4

Close on purpose

Name the decision, test or next action and stop reopening the topic.

Case A · “That won’t work. We already tried it.”

Weak reflex

Explain why your idea is good until the expert agrees.

Leadership move

Use the objection as data. Ask what failed, under what conditions, and whether those conditions still apply.

Why

You respect experience without allowing “we tried it” to become an automatic veto.

“I want to use that experience.” → validates expertise

“What specifically failed last time?” → asks for evidence

“Which of those conditions are still true today?” → tests transferability

“Then we can decide whether the same conclusion still applies.” → preserves decision ownership

Case B · “You don’t have enough technical experience to decide that.”

Weak reflex

Defend your background, years of experience or legitimacy.

Leadership move

Agree that technical input must be strong, then define your management role in the decision process.

Why

You refuse the false choice between technical expert and credible manager.

Level 1: “I’m not replacing your technical expertise. I need it. My role is to make sure we reach a clear decision with the right inputs.”

Level 2: “I agree that the technical input must come from the strongest expertise in the room. My responsibility is to make sure the evidence is explicit, the risks are visible and a decision is made. So let’s identify the technical criteria that should drive that decision.”

Case C · When someone monopolises the meeting

Communication fact: interrupting to protect the purpose of a meeting is not the same as interrupting because you dislike what somebody is saying.

PURPOSE → PAUSE → ONE OUTPUT → MOVE

“I’m going to pause you there because I want to protect the decision time. Give us the one conclusion and the one risk you believe we cannot ignore, then I’ll bring in the other views.”

Professional debate or behaviour problem?

09 · Situation 5

Giving feedback without turning it into a personal judgement

France lens Direct critique can be more culturally acceptable, but effective feedback still needs observable facts, a clear impact and a concrete expectation.
OBSERVATION → IMPACT → EXPECTATION → CHECK-BACK

Example

“In the last two meetings, the discussion continued after the decision point and we lost time for the remaining topics. From now on, I need your recommendation first, then the detail if we need it. Let’s use that format in the next two meetings and review whether it improves the discussion.”

Why it works

The feedback is not “you talk too much.” It identifies a repeatable behaviour, the effect on the team, the expected replacement behaviour and a review point.

10 · Situation 6

A new colleague arrives with a history of conflict

Management rule

Manage the behaviour you observe, not the reputation you inherited.

A · Fact

You have information, but not your own evidence yet

Knowing there was conflict before can help you prepare, but it should not become the opening accusation in the new relationship.

B · Risk

Two traps

  • Pre-judging: the person senses that you expect trouble.
  • Overcompensating: you avoid clear expectations because you do not want to seem hostile.
C · Strategy

FRESH START → EXPECTATIONS → DISAGREEMENT RULE → EARLY CHECK-IN

Give a fair start and make the working rules explicit from day one.

D · Why it works

You create fairness and accountability together

The new colleague is not judged in advance, but the behavioural expectations are already clear.

Build your first conversation

Week 2 scenario

A new team member says: “I’m not going to waste time explaining something obvious.”

1

Name the boundary

Disagreement is allowed; dismissing a colleague is not.

2

Return to the work

Ask for the technical point and the evidence.

3

Protect participation

Make sure the other person is heard.

4

Follow up

If needed, address the behaviour privately afterwards.

Level 1: “You may disagree, but we need to keep the discussion respectful. Explain the technical point without dismissing the person.”

Level 2: “I’m comfortable with strong disagreement; I’m not comfortable with dismissing a colleague. Keep the challenge on the technical point, explain the evidence, and let’s make sure the other view is heard before we decide.”

14 · Communication card

Six rules to carry into the new role

1. Do not confuse direct debate with personal conflict. First test the reasoning.

2. Respect expertise and hierarchy without becoming passive.

3. When an explanation is too broad, value the context before narrowing the question.

4. A knowledge-transfer gap should be described as an operational risk, not a personal failure.

5. Manage difficult people through observable behaviour, explicit expectations and regular feedback.

6. Your role is not to know everything. Your role is to make expertise, risk, ownership and decisions clear.
