01 · Flight plan

The next step: from prepared briefing to live executive fluency

You have already worked on message structure, natural delivery and turning technical detail into a decision-ready briefing. This lesson adds the skill that makes those tools usable in a real room: staying clear and connected when you have not planned the exact sentence in advance.

FR : Vous avez déjà travaillé la structure du message, la prise de parole naturelle et la transformation d’un contenu technique en briefing orienté décision. Cette leçon ajoute la compétence qui permet d’utiliser ces outils en situation réelle : rester claire et connectée lorsque la phrase exacte n’a pas été préparée à l’avance.

1

Think without disappearing

Replace automatic fillers with a deliberate pause, a clear bridge or one short thinking phrase.

2

Control pace

Use speed as a leadership choice: brisk for context, slower for risk, pause before the recommendation.

3

Stay visually connected

Use eye contact at the moments that carry meaning instead of trying to maintain an artificial stare.

4

Answer live

Use cue words rather than memorised sentences to respond to management questions in your own voice.

Mission principle

A pause is not a loss of fluency. A pause can show control.

The goal is not to speak continuously. The goal is to make the next sentence worth hearing.

03 · Fluency control

Keep the connection while you think

The objective is not to ban every hesitation sound. It is to stop an automatic chain of “um… um…” from taking control of the message.

A

Silent pause

Finish the idea. Breathe. Keep your face engaged. Start the next idea when it is ready.

2-second pause The main risk is traceability. Look up The second point is the schedule impact.

B

Signpost bridge

Use a structure phrase that tells the audience where you are going.

“There are two points I would separate here.”

“The key point for me is…”

C

One thinking phrase

Use sparingly when you genuinely need a moment. Then answer.

“Let me think about the best way to frame that.”

“I would separate the short-term and long-term impact.”

Avoid the trap: do not replace “um” with a new repeated filler such as “you know”, “so”, “actually” or “basically”. The aim is space + structure, not a different verbal habit.

04 · Pace laboratory

Speed is not one setting

A strong executive speaker changes speed according to purpose. The audience should feel a difference between context, risk and decision.

↗

Brisk

Known context, transitions, low-risk detail.

≈

Natural

Main explanation and evidence.

↓

Slow + pause

Risk, consequence, recommendation, decision.

Which delivery choice is strongest?

“My recommendation is to delay the decision until the evidence is complete.”

Compression practice

Same idea, three time envelopes

Your scenario: the project is still on track, the main validation work is complete, one cross-team interface still needs evidence, and you recommend closing it this week before milestone approval.

Trainer focus: the message must stay the same. Only the amount of supporting detail changes. If a detail does not change the decision, risk, evidence or next action, it does not belong in the first answer.

20 secHeadline + risk/condition + action 45 secHeadline + 1–2 evidence points + risk + action 75 secHeadline + selective context + evidence + impact + action
20 secDecision level

LOOK The project remains on track, but one interface still needs final evidence. PAUSE LAND I recommend closing it this week before milestone approval.

Why this works: no history, no technical mechanism, no list of teams—only what management needs to decide.

45 secEvidence level

LOOK Overall, the project remains on track. We have completed the main validation review and have closed most of the open points. PAUSE One cross-team interface still needs final evidence, so I would keep that condition visible. LAND I recommend a focused closure review this week before milestone approval.

Added: two useful evidence points. Still omitted: detailed chronology, component-level explanation and every contributor.

75 secSelective detail

LOOK My current assessment is that the planned milestone remains achievable. Since the last review, we have completed the core validation activities, clarified the main requirements questions and strengthened the evidence position. SLOW One cross-functional interface has not yet reached the evidence level we need for final approval. It is not blocking the work today, but carrying it into the milestone would reduce decision confidence. PAUSE LAND I recommend keeping the target date and making interface closure a clear condition for approval.

Added: consequence and selective context. Still omitted: information that is technically true but does not alter the management decision.

The detail-stop question

Before adding another fact, ask yourself: “Will this fact change their understanding of the decision, the risk, the evidence or the action?”

If the answer is no, park it for Q&A. You are not hiding information—you are sequencing it.

06 · Grammar refresh

Present perfect for project status — quick reminder

This is a refresher, not a grammar detour. The tense is useful because executive updates constantly connect what has happened to where things stand now.

have / has+past participle

Current result

We have completed the first validation phase.

Result matters now.

Change / development

We have identified two new dependencies.

New information changes the current picture.

Not finished yet

We haven’t closed the compliance point yet.

The status is still open now.

Finished time → past simple

We completed the review last Friday.

“Last Friday” is a finished time.

FR : Present perfect = lien entre le passé et la situation actuelle. Prétérit = événement situé dans un moment passé terminé. Ex. We have completed the review (résultat actuel) / We completed it last Friday (date passée terminée).

08 · Live response architecture

Answer from cue words, not a script

When you are asked an unexpected question, use a small mental map. It gives you direction without forcing you to remember a paragraph.

A

Answer

Give the direct headline first.

“Yes, but with one condition.”
E

Evidence

Give one reason, fact or constraint.

“We have completed the main review, but one interface remains open.”
N

Next

Say what should happen now.

“I recommend closing that interface before the decision.”
Only remember three letters: A → E → N. The wording changes every time. That is exactly the point.

10 · Executive compression

Important does not mean “include it now”

In engineering, many facts are genuinely important. Executive communication requires a second judgement: which facts are important for this audience, at this moment, for this decision?

The 1–3–1 rule

1 headline → maximum 3 supporting facts → 1 action / decision request.

Everything else can remain available for Q&A. Concision is not the removal of rigour; it is the organisation of rigour.

KEEP

Changes the decision

Risk, evidence maturity, schedule impact, compliance implication, recommendation, required action.

PARK

Useful if they ask

Technical mechanism, chronology, team-by-team detail, secondary figures, how the evidence was produced.

REMOVE

Does not help this answer

Repeated background, side issues, every discussion point, information already known by the audience.

Management asks: “Can we keep the milestone date?”

Which information belongs in your first answer?

You are explaining one remaining interface risk.

Which detail should normally be parked for Q&A?

Your audience already knows the project background.

Which opening is strongest?

You have already given the headline, evidence and recommendation.

What is the strongest next move?

Engineer-to-executive practice

Start with all the information. Then decide what earns a place.

The interface issue was first raised during the Tuesday working session after the team compared the current evidence set with the updated requirement interpretation. Three engineers from Validation and Verification, two system representatives and one programme representative reviewed the available test results, the open assumptions and several possible closure paths. The missing input is not preventing current engineering activity, but it could leave the milestone decision insufficiently supported if it is not closed before approval. The current proposal is to organise a focused cross-team review this week, confirm one owner and close the evidence package before the milestone.

How to correct this skill

  1. Ask for the headline: Can you understand the answer from sentence 1?
  2. Count support: Are you using no more than three facts before the action?
  3. Challenge each extra detail: “Does this change the decision, risk, evidence or action?”
  4. Reward stopping: once the recommendation is clear, silence is a successful ending—not missing content.
  5. Only then correct language: grammar matters, but relevance and hierarchy are the communication target.

12 · Oral transfer

From written clarity to spoken ownership

Use your cue words, not the paragraph. Your answer may be shorter, simpler or differently ordered. It should sound like you.

How to run and correct this section

Before speaking

Give yourself 10–15 seconds to write a maximum of four cue words. No sentences.

While speaking

Headline first. Maximum three supporting facts. Recommendation/next step. Then stop.

If interrupted

Answer the interruption directly. Do not restart. Return to the next cue word.

If you know more

Do not prove everything you know. Keep extra technical information available for the trainer’s follow-up question.

Trainer correction order: 1) relevance / detail selection → 2) structure → 3) pace & fillers → 4) eye contact → 5) language accuracy. Correcting grammar first can hide the real communication issue.

Round 1 · 60 seconds

Give the project update without reading.

Mission: management wants a current status before the next milestone.

  1. Headline
  2. What has changed / been completed
  3. One remaining issue only
  4. Recommendation

Stop rule: when the recommendation is clear, stop. The trainer can ask for detail.

Round 2 · Interruption

Trainer interrupts: “Why is this still open?”

Response map: A → E → N

  1. Answer why in sentence 1.
  2. Give one cause/evidence point.
  3. State what will close it.

Do not: return to the beginning or explain the full history.

Round 3 · Compression

“I only have 20 seconds. What do you need from me?”

Only three elements:

  1. Decision/request
  2. One reason
  3. Required action / condition

Success: the listener knows exactly what you need before the timer ends.

13 · CLOE transfer

Professional questions without a prepared script

The exam transfer is deliberate: listen, take a micro-pause, answer directly, develop one idea, then stop cleanly.

Your CLOE response protocol

LISTEN → MICRO-PAUSE → DIRECT ANSWER → ONE developed idea/example → CLEAN STOP

Preparation

Write 3–4 cue words only. Never prepare a paragraph.

Target length

Aim for roughly 35–50 seconds unless the question naturally needs less.

Detail control

Give one example or reason. A second example is optional only if it adds something new.

How to finish

Finish the idea and stop. Do not keep talking simply because you know more.

Trainer scoring lens: Was the question answered immediately? Was the development relevant? Was the amount of detail controlled? Did the answer have a clear ending? Then assess vocabulary, grammar, pronunciation and fluency.

Question 3 · Disagreement

What do you do when a colleague disagrees with your recommendation?

Answer mapListenClarify reasonCompare evidenceAgree next step

Keep it behavioural: what you do, not a long story about one disagreement.

Models + trainer correction

B2

First, I try to understand why the colleague disagrees instead of defending my position immediately. I ask questions, compare the available evidence and make sure we are using the same criteria. If the disagreement remains, I focus on the project objective and the risk behind each option. The goal is to reach a decision that is supported by facts, not by hierarchy.

C1

I treat disagreement as useful information, particularly in an engineering environment. I first clarify whether we disagree on the facts, the criteria or the acceptable level of risk. Once that is visible, we can compare the evidence against the project objective and decide what needs further validation. That usually turns a personal disagreement into a technical decision process.

Correct for: a repeatable method, logical connectors and a clean conclusion. If the example becomes long, reduce it to one sentence.

Question 4 · Learning

Answer mapSituationDifficultyLearningWhat you do now

Models + trainer correction

B2

I have learned that giving more information does not always make a message clearer. In one difficult situation, several technical issues were being discussed at the same time and the audience started to lose the main point. Since then, I have tried to identify the decision first, explain only the evidence that supports it and keep the remaining detail for questions.

C1

One important lesson I have learned is that technical completeness and communication completeness are not the same thing. In a high-pressure situation, I realised that giving every relevant fact at once can make the decision less visible. I now separate what the audience needs to decide from what they may need to investigate, and I sequence the detail accordingly.

Correct for: present perfect in the learning statement, one concise situation, and a specific change in current behaviour. Do not let the background become longer than the learning.
