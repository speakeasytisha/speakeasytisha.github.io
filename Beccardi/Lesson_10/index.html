Grammar workshops — learn, apply, then move on

Study one rule at a time. Check meaning, affirmative, negative and question forms, time markers and common errors. Each grammar point has its own mini-task and two model sentences.

Grammar 1 / 4

Present simple — current habits

When / why: Use for a routine, frequency or general fact.

+ AffirmativeI/we + base verb; he/she + verb-s.

− NegativeI/we do not + base; he/she does not + base.

? QuestionDo/Does + subject + base?

Time markers: usually, often, every Sunday, from time to time

Exception / common error: He sees his family — but Does he see his family? Frequency: I usually visit, but I am usually free.

In context: We usually get together when we can.

Try it immediately: Describe a regular family habit.
B1 example

We often have lunch together on Sundays.

B1+/B2 example

We try to get together whenever everyone is available, even if our schedules are busy.

Grammar 2 / 4

Past simple — a finished occasion

When / why: Use for an event finished at a known time.

+ AffirmativeSubject + past form: organised / went / had.

− NegativeSubject + did not + base form.

? QuestionDid + subject + base form?

Time markers: last weekend, yesterday, in October, two weeks ago

Exception / common error: French passé composé often becomes English past simple with a finished-time marker. NOT We have organised last month.

In context: Last month, we organised a family lunch.

Try it immediately: Describe something you did together last month.
B1 example

Last month, we had lunch together.

B1+/B2 example

Last month, we arranged a family meal and spent the afternoon catching up.

Grammar 3 / 4

Used to — past habits that changed

When / why: Use for a former habit or situation, not one single past event.

+ AffirmativeSubject + used to + base.

− NegativeSubject + did not use to + base.

? QuestionDid + subject + use to + base?

Time markers: when I was younger, years ago, before, nowadays

Exception / common error: Used to ≠ be used to: I used to travel (former habit), I am used to travelling (familiar with it).

In context: We used to meet more often, but now everyone is busier.

Try it immediately: Explain a family habit that was different in the past.
B1 example

We used to meet every weekend.

B1+/B2 example

We used to see one another more frequently, whereas nowadays we have to plan ahead.

Grammar 4 / 4

Present perfect — life up to now

When / why: Use for experiences or changes with a connection to now, without a completed past-time reference.

+ AffirmativeSubject + have/has + past participle.

− NegativeSubject + have/has not + past participle.

? QuestionHave/Has + subject + past participle?

Time markers: already, yet, ever, never, recently, over the years

Exception / common error: Do not use with last Sunday / in 2023. Have you ever hosted? Yes, I hosted last year.

In context: We have celebrated several important occasions together.

Try it immediately: Mention an experience you have had with your family.
B1 example

We have travelled together a few times.

B1+/B2 example

Over the years, we have managed to keep several traditions alive despite our changing schedules.

French vs English: notice the construction, not just the translation

The purpose is to notice which tense, word order and expression English actually uses. You do not need to translate every thought before speaking.

French: Nous essayons de nous retrouver dès que tout le monde est libre.

Natural English: We try to get together whenever everyone is available.

What changes? “Nous essayons” → “we try” (present habit); “de nous retrouver” becomes “to get together” (try to + base verb); “dès que” can naturally become whenever for repeated occasions. English uses everyone is (singular), not “everyone are”.

French: Le mois dernier, nous avons organisé un repas en famille.

Natural English: Last month, we organised a family meal.

What changes? French passé composé (“avons organisé”) describes the finished event. English uses past simple with “last month,” not present perfect. We say “a family meal,” not “a meal in family”. Both “organised” (UK) and “organized” (US) are correct.

Grammar 3 / 4

Present perfect — experience until now

When / why: Use to say whether you have ever had an experience; no specific finished time yet.

+ AffirmativeI have been to… / I have seen…

− NegativeI have never attended…

? QuestionHave you ever been to…?

+ AffirmativeA is more exciting than B.

− NegativeA is not as relaxing as B.

− NegativeI did not join just to…

Grammar 1 / 4

Descriptive verbs — seems / smells / tastes / feels

When / why: Use a sense verb plus an adjective; an aroma is often introduced with of.

+ AffirmativeIt smells fruity. It has a hint of spice.

− NegativeIt does not taste very sweet.

? QuestionDoes it taste dry or sweet?

Time markers: on the nose, on the palate, at first

Exception / common error: Say smells fruity (adjective), not smells fruit; smells of cherries (noun).

In context: On the nose, it smells of cherries. On the palate, it feels smooth.

Try it immediately: Describe one aroma and one mouthfeel.
B1 example

It smells fruity and feels smooth.

B1+/B2 example

It has pronounced aromas of red berries, while the texture on the palate is particularly smooth.

Grammar 2 / 4

Comparatives and degree

When / why: Make an observation rather than call something simply good or bad.

+ AffirmativeA is lighter / more aromatic than B.

− NegativeA is not as full-bodied as B.

? QuestionIs it drier than the first wine?

Time markers: slightly, a little, much, compared with, whereas

Exception / common error: Dry → drier; full-bodied → more full-bodied; good → better.

In context: This one is slightly lighter than the first wine.

Try it immediately: Compare two wines or two styles.
B1 example

This wine is lighter than the last one.

B1+/B2 example

Compared with the previous glass, this wine is a little more aromatic but noticeably lighter on the palate.

Grammar 3 / 4

Hedging and polite opinion — seems / might / could

When / why: A tasting impression is subjective. Express uncertainty without sounding unsure of yourself.

+ AffirmativeI would say… / It seems… / It could pair…

− NegativeI would not say it is especially sweet.

? QuestionWould you say it tastes dry?

Time markers: I think, to me, I would say, perhaps, might

Exception / common error: Could / might + base verb; do not say could to pair.

In context: I’d say it could pair well with roast chicken.

Try it immediately: Suggest a pairing without making an absolute claim.
B1 example

I think it could go well with fish.

B1+/B2 example

I’d say the freshness could make it a good match for grilled fish, although it depends on the sauce.

Grammar 4 / 4

Relative clauses — give useful detail

When / why: Add a short explanation so non-experts can follow.

+ AffirmativeThis is a wine that comes from…

− NegativeIt is not a style that everyone enjoys.

? QuestionIs it a wine that you would serve with food?

Time markers: which, that, who, where

Exception / common error: Who = people; which/that = things. Do not add an extra pronoun: a wine that it comes from…

In context: This is a wine that comes from a cooler region.

Try it immediately: Explain one wine term in simple English.
B1 example

Tannins are what make some red wines feel dry in your mouth.

B1+/B2 example

Tannins, which are found particularly in red wines, can create a drying sensation on the palate.

Grammar 1 / 4

Open questions — natural conversations

When / why: Follow up with how, what, why or which rather than only yes/no.

+ AffirmativeWhat do you enjoy? / How did you start?

− NegativeWhat do you not enjoy as much?

? QuestionWhat + do/does/did + subject + base?

Time markers: how, what, why, which, how long

Exception / common error: After did, use base: How did you become interested? Not became.

In context: What do you enjoy most about your association?

Try it immediately: Ask a genuine question about a colleague’s interest.
B1 example

How did you get interested in that?

B1+/B2 example

What first sparked your interest, and what keeps you involved now?

Grammar 2 / 4

Indirect questions — professional politeness

When / why: Use a polite introductory clause + statement word order.

+ AffirmativeCould you tell me when the meeting starts?

− NegativeI am not sure when the meeting starts.

? QuestionCould you explain why the reading changed?

Time markers: Could you tell me…, Do you know…, I was wondering…

Exception / common error: NOT Could you tell me when does it start? No inversion after when in the embedded clause.

In context: Could you clarify which measurement is outside tolerance?

Try it immediately: Ask for a technical clarification politely.
B1 example

Could you explain which part we need to check?

B1+/B2 example

Could you walk us through the measurements that led to this conclusion?

Grammar 3 / 4

Reported facts vs uncertainty

When / why: Separate what was measured from a possible cause.

+ AffirmativeThe result shows… / The fixture may be involved.

− NegativeWe have not confirmed the cause.

? QuestionCould the variation be related to the fixture?

Time markers: according to, appears to, may, might, not yet

Exception / common error: Might/may/could + base verb; don’t claim cause without evidence.

In context: Two measurements are outside tolerance; the cause is not yet confirmed.

Try it immediately: Say one fact, one hypothesis and one proposed action.
B1 example

Two readings are high. The fixture may be involved. Let’s check again.

B1+/B2 example

The initial readings are outside tolerance; the fixture may be contributing, but we should repeat the measurements before drawing a conclusion.

Grammar 4 / 4

First conditional — agreed next step

When / why: Use for a realistic future condition.

+ AffirmativeIf + present, will/can + base.

− NegativeIf we do not receive them, we will wait.

? QuestionWhat will we do if the results change?

Time markers: if, unless, as soon as, once

Exception / common error: No will in ordinary if-clause: If we receive, not If we will receive.

In context: If the next check confirms it, we can decide on the next action.

Try it immediately: Explain the plan depending on a future result.
B1 example

If the measurements are correct, we will move forward.

B1+/B2 example

If the follow-up readings confirm the variation, we can agree on a corrective action and communicate the decision to the team.

Wine tasting: describe, don't overwhelm

1 · Appearance

Colour and intensity: pale gold, ruby, garnet; clear, deep

2 · Nose

Aroma: citrus, floral, red berries, dark fruit, herbs, spice

3 · Palate

Dryness, acidity, tannins, body, texture: crisp, smooth, balanced

4 · Finish

What remains: short, medium or lingering; fruity or spicy

5 · Compare & connect

Lighter than; more aromatic; food pairing; personal preference

Two-level speaking model

B1: This wine is ruby-red. It smells of cherries and a little spice. It feels smooth and has a pleasant finish. I think it could go well with chicken. What do you think?

B1+/B2: It has a deep ruby colour and quite pronounced red-fruit aromas, particularly cherry. On the palate, it's medium-bodied, with lively acidity, soft tannins and a fairly persistent finish. Compared with the previous glass, this one feels rounder. I'd consider pairing it with roast poultry. Do you notice the same fruit notes?

Descriptive examples are illustrative, not facts about a particular bottle. Use only the characteristics you actually observe.
