01 · WRITING DNA · 7 MIN

Good professional writing is not “advanced English.” It is useful English that cannot be misunderstood.

For CLOE, you need to be able to write clear, professional English for realistic workplace situations. The exact situation can change, so your goal is to communicate successfully in different professional formats—not to memorise one model.

Purposethe reader immediately understands why you are writing
Contentall essential facts, actions, dates and conditions are included
Structureinformation follows a logical, readable order
Registertone fits the relationship and professional context
Accuracygrammar and vocabulary support meaning without ambiguity
i
What you need to know

Be ready to write different types of professional communication, including professional emails, technical notes, meeting summaries/reports, problem or incident updates, instructions and procedures, internal messages, handover notes and recommendations. Focus on the skill behind every task: understand what is required, include all essential information, organise it clearly, choose the right professional tone, use accurate English and check that nothing could be misunderstood.

02 · C.L.E.A.R. METHOD · 10 MIN

Five moves for almost any professional writing task.

Do not start typing as soon as you see the prompt. A 20-second plan often prevents the errors that cost the most clarity.

C

Context

Who are you writing to and why?

Client? colleague? manager? supplier? HR?
L

List

Extract every required piece of information.

Date, action, reason, request, attachment, deadline…
E

Establish

Put the information in a logical order.

Purpose → essential details → action → close.
A

Adjust

Choose language and register for the reader.

Direct but polite. Clear, not translated word-for-word.
R

Review

Check meaning before grammar perfection.

Could the reader misunderstand anything?
COACH THINKING · MODEL

Task: Reply to a supplier. The delivery is late. Ask for a new confirmed date and explain that production may be affected.

CContext: external supplier → professional, polite, clear.
LList: late delivery + request confirmed date + possible production impact.
EEstablish: problem first, request second, impact third, close with expected action.
AAdjust: “Could you please confirm…” is more appropriate than “Tell me when…”
RReview: Is the requested action obvious? Is the deadline/date precise?
TRAP 01

French structure

Avoid translating sentence order literally. English professional messages are often more direct and linear.

TRAP 02

Missing action

A polite message can still fail if the reader does not know what you need them to do next.

TRAP 03

Wrong register

“I want you to…” may be grammatically correct but too abrupt in many professional situations.

TRAP 04

Ambiguous pronouns

Words such as it, this, they can become unclear when several documents, dates or people are mentioned.

TRAP 05

Overwriting

Longer is not better. Every sentence should help the reader understand or act.

5 final questions

  1. Did I answer every instruction?
  2. Are dates/numbers exact?
  3. Is the action clear?
  4. Is the register appropriate?
  5. Could anything be misunderstood?
WRITING TYPE MASTERCLASS · DIFFERENT PROFESSIONAL FORMATS

Do not prepare for “an email.” Prepare to communicate in different professional formats.

Explore the purpose, structure, coaching, scenario and two model levels for each format. Do not memorise the models word for word—learn the structure and useful language so you can adapt to a new situation.

04 · GUIDED WRITING LAB · 22 MIN

Control the building blocks—then produce the whole message.

The first tasks are automatically corrected. Then practise complete professional writing through emails, internal updates, incident reports, meeting summaries, technical notes, procedures, handovers and recommendations. Use the first four for the 60-minute core lesson and continue the others for extra practice or homework. Each task includes a clear checklist, coaching and two model levels.
